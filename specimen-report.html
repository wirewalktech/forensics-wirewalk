Why this page exists

Every guide written for lawyers buying forensic services says the same thing: before you instruct an examiner, ask to see a report. Not a brochure and not a tool list. A report, in the format that would be served in your matter, so you can see whether the findings are traceable to evidence or merely asserted over it.

It is good advice, and almost nobody can act on it. A real report belongs to a client and cannot be sent to a stranger. A redacted one usually loses the very structure you were asking about in the first place. So I publish the format instead, filled in with values that are obviously invented and marked on every block, so it can never drift into being read as an account of work I have done.

This is not a case study. I publish no case studies, and I name no client, matter, court or party. There is no testimonial slot in the template for anyone to fill in later. What I can show you is the shape of the work product. If you already know what a sound report looks like, you do not need a story about one.

2

What to look for in any examination report

The test is not whether the report sounds confident. It is whether another examiner, handed the same material, could follow the document to the same place, and whether the places the author could not reach are visible on the page. Six things carry most of that weight. In each of them, the absence tells you more than the presence does.

Reading an examination report: the six load-bearing parts
What to look forWhy it carries weightWhat its absence tells you
The question, stated as it was asked An examination answers an instruction. Written at the front, the question fixes the scope and makes it visible when a finding wanders outside it That scope is being set retrospectively by what happened to be found. A report with no stated question cannot be off-topic, which is not a virtue.
An inventory of material with identifiers and digests It says exactly what was examined, separates that from what was merely mentioned, and lets a second examiner verify they hold the same thing That the examined material can be identified at all. Without identifiers and digests, a later challenge to what was analysed cannot be answered, only re-asserted.
A method described to the level of tool and version Repeatability is not a slogan. It is whether the steps and the versions were written down while they were being performed That the work was reproducible. An unnamed tool and an undated version make a finding untestable rather than wrong, and untestable is where most of the argument happens.
Each finding tied to the artefacts that support it A finding is a claim plus its evidence. Keeping the two separate lets the reader see which parts of an opinion survive if one artefact falls That the reasoning can be checked. A narrative that reads well and cites nothing is an argument, and it will be cross-examined as one.
A stated confidence, in defined words Different findings in the same report deserve different weight, and the examiner is the person best placed to say which is which That the author distinguished between what was observed and what was inferred. Uniform certainty across every paragraph is itself a finding about the report.
Limits, stated per finding and for the examination as a whole The limits are what the other side will attack. Written before the engagement, they are method; written on the stand, they are a concession That the examiner has thought about what the evidence cannot carry. An examiner who has not written the limits down beforehand will be composing them under cross-examination.

These are the parts that get attacked, which is why they are the parts worth publishing. None of it is house style. Each one corresponds to something an opponent can put to the author in a deposition.

2.1

Four sentences that should not appear, in anyone's report

A device is not a person. So a finding that a named individual did something, resting on device artefacts alone, has crossed from evidence into attribution, and it needs a separately stated methodology if it is going to be offered at all.

Absence of an artefact is not evidence of absence. A sentence that treats a missing log entry as proof that nothing happened is unsupported on its face, because retention windows, log rotation, TRIM on solid-state media and ordinary system behaviour all destroy data with no intent behind them. Intent to destroy evidence is a finding for the court, so an examiner who asserts it has answered a question that was not theirs to answer. And a promise that material will be admitted is a promise about a decision the author does not get to make.

If I am asked to read the report served against you, those four are the first things I look for. They are the sentences most likely to be in it, and each one is reachable in cross-examination without any technical rebuttal at all.

3

Reading an opposing examiner's report

The same six parts do the work in the other direction. Where a report supplies them, the argument is technical and runs on the artefacts. Where it does not, the question is simpler: what would have to be produced before any of this could be checked, and has it been?

On a first-pass review I mark each material finding as supported, partly supported, unsupported, or untestable on the material provided, and set out the questions to put. The fourth category does most of the work and is the one most often misread. A finding is untestable where the underlying artefacts were not produced, where the method was not described in enough detail to reproduce, or where the examination itself altered or consumed what would be needed to check it. Untestable does not mean wrong. Counsel would usually prefer it to, and I do not let it become a synonym for wrong in my own writing either.

Read the other side's report for what it declines to claim, as well. A careful report from a competent opponent already contains its own limits, and those paragraphs are frequently the most useful thing in the document for the party that did not commission it.

4

What follows, and what it is not

Below is the report format I use, filled in. Every value in it is invented. The parties, the custodian, the devices, the identifiers, the dates and the digests were written for this page and correspond to nothing.

Read the specimen as a form, not as an account. No court or jurisdiction is named in it. The digests are not hexadecimal: they contain the letters P, M and N, which cannot appear in a SHA-256 value, so no line on this page can be mistaken for a real digest. Nothing on this page states or implies that the examination described took place, that I have been retained in any matter, or that any report has been served, tendered, admitted, deposed on or tested in any proceeding.

It runs long for a matter of this size, and that is on purpose. A short report is usually short because the limits were left out. Sections are numbered continuously with the rest of this page so you can point at any part of it precisely.

5.1

Specimen: the question asked

[SPECIMEN — NOT A REAL MATTER]

I was instructed in writing on 2026-02-24 to answer one question:

Whether the nine documents listed in Schedule A to the instruction were transferred from the laptop issued to Custodian A — "A. N. Placeholder" — to any removable device or non-corporate account between 2026-01-15 and 2026-02-20, and if so, by what mechanism and when.

Two things were expressly outside the instruction and were not examined: the content of the nine documents, and any question of the custodian's authority to hold them. One further thing was outside it by nature rather than by instruction, and is stated here because a reader will otherwise assume the report answers it. I was not asked, and this examination cannot say, who was physically at the keyboard at any moment described below.

Instruction received in writing. Where an instruction changes mid-examination, the change is recorded with its date and the report states which findings were produced under which instruction.

5.3

Specimen: method applied

[SPECIMEN — NOT A REAL MATTER]

The examination followed the published procedure at /method in the version current at the engagement date, which forms part of this report. It is written against the four principles in ISO/IEC 27037:2012 (auditability, repeatability, reproducibility and justifiability), with the phase structure in NIST SP 800-86. Those are guidance I work to, not accreditation I hold.

In a served report this clause names each tool with its version, the acquisition and verification steps in order with their timestamps, the artefact classes parsed, the search terms and filter criteria applied, and the hypotheses tested and discarded. I keep that material as the work happens, and on the assumption it will be produced, because FRCP 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) reaches facts and data considered, not merely relied upon.

Two method notes belong in the body rather than an appendix, because both bear on the weight of what follows. First, write blocking was used for Item 1 and could not be used for Item 2, which is a cloud source; for that item soundness rests on documenting the export method, recording the tool and version, hashing the container at the earliest possible point and stating what could not be preserved. Second, the artefact classes underlying Finding 2 have known interpretation variance across operating-system builds. The specific behaviour was therefore tested on a matching build before the finding was written, and the test is described in the exhibit.

Where an engagement requires a certified examiner, an accredited laboratory or a licence, that condition is stated in the engagement letter and satisfied before work begins.

5.4

Specimen: the confidence vocabulary used in this report

[SPECIMEN — NOT A REAL MATTER]

Four words are used with fixed meanings, defined here so that a reader is never left to infer weight from tone. Observed — present in the artefacts and directly readable, with the artefact identified. Supported — an inference drawn from two or more independent artefact classes that agree, with the inference stated. Consistent with — the artefacts do not contradict the proposition and do not establish it; other explanations are named. Not established — the material examined does not answer the question, whether because the artefact was never created, has been overwritten, or lies outside what was collected.

The fourth word is used as freely as the first three. A report in which nothing is not established is a report in which the examiner has not said where the evidence stops.

Findings 4 and 5 are in the table for the same reason Item 4 is in the inventory. An examination that reports only what it found, and stays quiet about the parts of the question it could not reach, leaves you to read that silence as a negative answer. It is not one. The report has to say so in its own voice, not in a footnote.

Finding 5 is where a report overreaches most easily, and it is the line a client would most like rewritten. What the artefacts support is a statement about tooling. It is not a statement about whether files were deleted, and it is certainly not a statement about anyone's intention. I report what was destroyed, when, and by what mechanism. Whether a party acted with intent to deprive another of evidence is a finding for the court under FRCP 37(e)(2), and I do not offer it.

Saying which exhibit is an illustrative aid and which one is substantive sounds like pedantry. It is not. A timeline is the most persuasive object a forensic report produces, and it is the one everybody in the room, on both sides, is most likely to start treating as though it were itself evidence. So I say which I am producing, in the exhibit list, before anyone has to ask.

5.7

Specimen: limitations of the examination as a whole

[SPECIMEN — NOT A REAL MATTER]

These apply to every finding above and are not repeated in each one.

Limitations

  1. Timestamps in this report are as recorded by the source system, converted to the stated offset. They depend on time-zone configuration, clock drift, filesystem semantics, application behaviour and network time state. Where two artefacts disagree, the disagreement is reported rather than reconciled silently.
  2. The examination reaches what was collected. Item 3 was a targeted collection, and what was deliberately not collected is listed in the manifest at Exhibit E-1.
  3. Nothing predates the earliest surviving log entry. The audit log at Item 2 reflects the tenant's retention configuration and licence tier at the time of export, and the record type showing which individual messages were read was not available at that tier. That is a limit on what could ever have been answered, not a limit on the search performed.
  4. The removable device at Item 4 was not produced. Every statement about it is a statement about records held on Item 1.
  5. No question of attribution to a person is addressed. Device and account evidence shows what a device or an account did. An opinion on who was at the keyboard would require a separately stated methodology and is not offered here.
  6. Deleted-data recovery is conditional on medium, filesystem and encryption, and is frequently unavailable on modern encrypted flash storage. Where recovery was attempted and failed, that is recorded at Exhibit E-1 rather than omitted.
  7. This report answers the question at clause 5.1 on the material at clause 5.2 as at the report date. If further material is produced, or if an assumption recorded here is shown to be wrong, the findings may change, and I will say so in writing.

The full published statement of what digital evidence cannot establish is at /limits. I wrote it before any engagement, rather than in response to one.

6

What changes between this specimen and a real report

The structure does not change. The length does, and mostly in one direction. The method clause and the exhibits get longer, because a real examination touches more artefact classes and every one of them brings its own interpretation question. The findings clause usually gets shorter. An examination that answers a narrow question honestly produces fewer findings than a client expects, and more "not established" entries than an advocate would like. Both of those are the format working rather than failing.

Two things get settled before the report is drafted rather than during it. The first is the role, which fixes what is disclosable; the four roles and their discovery posture are set out at /roles. The second is the authorised ceiling, which fixes what the examination can cost. I stop and report at eighty per cent of it and do not resume without a written increase, so a report is never quietly truncated by a budget you cannot see.

In any report I produce, I will not

  1. Write to a conclusion supplied in advance, or report an inference as a finding because a stronger letter is wanted.
  2. State a finding without the artefacts that support it and the confidence it is stated with.
  3. Leave a limit out of the report because it weakens it. The limits are written before the engagement; an examiner who has not written them down beforehand will be writing them on the stand.
  4. Opine on who was at the keyboard from device evidence alone. A device is not a person.
  5. Opine that a party acted with intent to destroy evidence. I report what the artefacts show. Intent is the court's finding, not mine.
  6. Present an illustrative aid as though it were substantive evidence, or a summary without the material it summarises.
  7. Promise you a finding, a recovered file, an unlocked handset, or that anything I produce will be admitted. Admissibility is the court's decision and no one can guarantee it.

The full list is on the home page and repeated verbatim in the engagement letter. My fee is the same whichever side retains me, it is fixed before I look at anything, and it does not move with what I find. A report that helps you and a report that does not are billed identically.
