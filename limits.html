1

Why this is a published page and not a disclaimer

Every finding has a reach. Beyond it the same artefact supports several explanations at once, and which one is true is not a question the artefact answers. The reach is not a caveat bolted onto a finding. It is part of the finding, and it is the part an opposing examiner spends their time on.

An examiner who has not written the limits of a method down before the engagement will be writing them for the first time under cross-examination, when the wording is no longer theirs to choose. The same limits appear as a clause attached to each finding in the report, in this language, and we will not remove them because a stronger letter is wanted.

This is also the answer to the question counsel asks early and rarely gets answered: if I pay for this, what will I actually have? Sometimes the answer is that the question as posed cannot be reached from the data. Better known on the first call than after the invoice — section 8 sets out how to reshape one so that it can be.

2

People, devices and accounts

The three most damaging overstatements in this field are versions of one mistake made at rising levels of ambition: reading a machine as a person, then reading an account as a person, then reading a mechanism as a motive.

2.1

A device is not a person

An examination recovers what the machine did: which processes ran, which files were opened, written or deleted, which volumes were attached, and under which session or service context. Every one of those is a record about the machine. The step to a named human lives outside the device — access control and camera records, a second device carried by the same person at the same time, a credential nobody else held, an admission. Where that material exists we can say whether the device record is consistent with it. Where it does not, the gap does not close by looking harder at the disk.

The competing explanations are ordinary: a shared workstation, an unlocked session left unattended, a remote support tool, a scheduled task running under a user context, malware operating under the interactive user's token. Each produces artefacts indistinguishable, on the device alone, from the person you have in mind sitting down and typing.

Where an engagement asks for an attribution opinion, the methodology is stated in writing before work starts and names the non-device material it depends on.

2.2

An account action is not a person either

Authentication records show that a credential was presented and accepted. A credential is a bearer instrument. Multi-factor authentication raises the cost of a false attribution considerably; it does not eliminate it. Approval fatigue, a replayed session token, a helpdesk reset, a delegated mailbox and a shared administrative account all produce clean, unremarkable logs.

The useful work on a disputed account action is comparative. We build a baseline from the account's own history — source addresses, device identifiers, authentication method, working hours, geography — and state how far the disputed event departs from it. That is a finding about consistency, not identity. A consistent event is not proof and an inconsistent one is not guilt.

2.3

Intent is a finding for the court

Artefacts record mechanism and sequence, not a state of mind. We can often establish that material was destroyed, when, and by what means — a single deletion, a bulk removal, a wipe utility with a recognisable signature, a factory reset, the expiry of a retention rule — and whether a policy in force would have deleted the same material on the same day whether or not anybody wanted it gone.

Where routine expiry and deliberate destruction are indistinguishable in the artefacts, we report that they cannot be separated, and the fact that a different answer would have suited the instructing party does not change it. Under Rule 37(e)(2) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure the severest measures for lost electronically stored information are available only on a finding that the party acted with the intent to deprive another party of the information's use in the litigation — a threshold that negligence, and even gross negligence, do not reach. That finding belongs to the court. We supply what existed, what was removed, when, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored from backups, shadow copies, journals or a second custodian.

3

Time, and the several clocks involved

Timestamps are the most persuasive thing in a forensic report and the most routinely misread. They persuade because they are precise. Precision and accuracy are different properties, and nothing in the record announces which one you are looking at.

3.1

There is never only one clock

A single event can involve the hardware clock, the operating system clock as disciplined by a time service between synchronisations, the clock of the application writing the record, the clock of a server at the other end of the connection, and the assumptions of whatever tool normalises all of it afterwards. A workstation whose time was set wrong, or set back deliberately, writes the wrong time with no flag saying so. Where a second, independently generated record covers the same event we can test one clock against another. Where there is none, the time is single-source and we label it that way rather than letting it stand as though corroborated.

3.2

Zones, epochs and conversions

NTFS stores times in UTC; FAT and exFAT store local time, so the same file copied between them changes its apparent time by the offset with nothing wrong at either end. Application databases store seconds since 1970, milliseconds since 1970, microseconds since 1601, or seconds since 2001, as their designers chose. A tool assuming the wrong one does not produce an obvious error. It produces a plausible wrong date, which is worse. We publish times in ISO 8601 with an explicit offset — 2026-03-14T09:41:07-04:00 — and state the conversion applied and the field it came from. A time printed without an offset should be treated as unverified wherever you find it.

3.3

What filesystem times do and do not mean

"Created" does not mean authored. On a copy the creation time is the time of the copy while the modification time is usually inherited, which makes files routinely appear to have been modified before they existed. Sync clients, archive extraction, restores and indexing all move times. Last-access updating is deferred or disabled by default on many Windows configurations, so an absent access time means very little.

On NTFS each file carries two sets of times, in $STANDARD_INFORMATION and in $FILE_NAME. Tools that alter timestamps have historically written the first and not the second, which is why disagreement between them is informative — though current tooling often writes both, so agreement proves nothing in return. Where time matters, we look for an independent record of the event rather than the file's own account of itself.

4

Absence

Absence is where the most confident wrong conclusions in this field are reached, and in both directions at once: by treating a gap in the record as proof that nothing happened, and by treating it as proof that something was hidden. It can be made to carry weight, but only on stated conditions, and they are set out below.

4.1

Absence of an artefact is not evidence of absence

A thing that is not there may never have existed. It may have been removed by a person. It may have been removed by a retention job, a log rotation, a background trim cycle on solid-state storage, an upgrade that discarded an old database, or a reimage. Those are ordinary behaviours running on a schedule nobody thinks about, and several destroy data more thoroughly than a person trying to. An examination separates those explanations only where some record survives that does the separating — and that record is frequently the missing thing itself. Where it is, the honest finding is that the question cannot be resolved from this material, and we write it that way rather than presenting silence as a result.

4.2

What a missing artefact can mean

A narrower positive result is available and worth asking for by name. Where a system reliably creates a particular artefact for a class of event, and that artefact is present for comparable events in the same period but absent for the one in dispute, the absence carries weight. That argument requires stating how consistently the artefact is created, how long it survives, whether it can be suppressed and by whom, and how large the comparison set is. We state all four, because a bare assertion that "there would have been a record" does not survive contact with an opposing examiner.

The other honest use of absence is the boundary of the record. Nothing predates the earliest surviving log. Where the retention window opens after the events of interest, the answer to "did this happen" is "the record does not reach that far back" — not the same answer as "no", and never to be reported as though it were.

5

Particular artefacts and how far they reach

These are the artefacts that get asked about by name, and that opposing reports lean on. Each is genuinely useful, and each reaches less far than its reputation.

5.1

USB records, and evidence of copying

Windows records removable devices attached to a machine. The USBSTOR keys hold a vendor string, a product string and an instance identifier, with connection times available from associated keys and from setupapi.dev.log; a per-user MountPoints2 key associates a profile with a volume. Together these are good evidence that a device reporting a particular identity was presented to that machine, and often when.

They do not record that any file was copied, in either direction, or how much. They do not identify the physical device with certainty: the serial string is supplied by the device itself, inexpensive controllers repeat them across production runs, and where the second character of the instance identifier is an ampersand the string was generated by Windows precisely because the device supplied none. They do not identify who attached it. Evidence of copying comes from other artefacts with their own limits, and how much data was written to a removable device is generally not recoverable from the host at all.

Where a copy is recorded, what is recorded is the operation and not the reason for it. A file arriving in a personal cloud folder, on an external disk, or in a second account is produced identically by a deliberate act and by a sync client, a backup agent, a roaming profile or a mail rule doing what it was configured to do months earlier. Separating the two needs the configuration that was in force at the time and a pattern across many files and dates, not a single copy event — and where that configuration cannot be established from the material, the two explanations are not separable, which is what we report.

5.2

Deleted data, and why recovery is conditional

Deletion is not a software state that can be reversed by wanting it enough. On a conventional hard disk, deleting a file commonly unlinks it while the content remains in unallocated space until something reuses it, and recovery there is routine. On solid-state storage the controller is told the blocks are free and erases them on its own schedule, independently of the operating system; by the time a drive reaches an examiner the content is frequently gone at the hardware level, and no tool, budget or effort returns it. Full-disk encryption removes the possibility another way: unallocated space is ciphertext with no structure to carve.

The same conditionality applies inside applications. Messages and browser history live in databases that mark a deleted row free and keep it until the space is reused — until a vacuum operation rewrites the file, which several applications perform automatically with no user involvement. Backups, shadow copies, a synchronised second device and the counterparty's own copy of the same message are frequently better prospects than the medium the file was deleted from.

Before you pay for an attempt we set out what the medium and its encryption state permit, and which other source is the better prospect. That is a statement about conditions, not a prediction of the result: an attempt that recovers nothing bills exactly as one that recovers everything.

5.3

Cloud logs record access, not content

A cloud audit record says an operation of a stated type was performed against a named object at a stated time by a named principal. It does not contain the message, the document, or the version as it stood at that moment. Whether content can be produced is answered by retention, versioning, licence tier and whether a hold was in place — not by the log.

Two gaps are common enough to be worth checking in your own tenant today rather than during an incident, against your own licence and your provider's current documentation, because both move. In Microsoft 365, MailItemsAccessed — the record showing which messages were actually accessed, and therefore the record that governs what has to be notified — depends on audit licence tier and on the audit configuration in force at the time, and is absent from many tenants that assume they have it. In AWS, CloudTrail data events, which record object-level access, are not enabled by default and are billed separately when they are. Enabling either during an incident logs forward only and recovers nothing. Audit pipelines also throttle and truncate under load, so a quiet period in a log can be a quiet period in the pipeline.

5.4

Encrypted, partial and defended data

An encrypted volume without a key or recovery credential is not openable by effort, and the money is better spent locating the key. Mobile and cloud acquisitions are logical and partial by nature: what is obtainable depends on the handset, the operating system version, the state the device was in when seized, and the tooling available that month, and it moves in both directions over time. A result achieved on one device last year is not a promise about this one, which is why deeper mobile extraction is quoted only after the handset is inspected. Anti-forensic tooling exists, it is freely available, and it sometimes works; its use sometimes leaves recognisable traces and sometimes leaves none. Where we find traces we report them as what they are; where we find none we do not offer that as proof that nothing was done.

6

Automated output is not a finding

Forensic suites present interpretations as facts, because a column headed "USB inserted" reads better than a column headed "our current parse of a partly documented structure". Different tools parse the same structure differently, behaviour changes between versions, and two reputable products disagreeing about one artefact is unremarkable. That disagreement is information, and the tool version is part of the finding.

For any finding that matters we read behind the output to the underlying structure, record the tool and version, and state what was examined directly rather than accepted from a report pane. Carved data needs particular care: it arrives with no filename, no path and no filesystem timestamp, carrying only what the file itself contains, and a carved fragment tabulated beside ordinary files implies a context it does not have. One of the requirements the federal rule on expert testimony sets — Rule 702(d) of the Federal Rules of Evidence — is that the opinion reflects a reliable application of the principles and methods to the facts of the case. An output nobody has read behind is not an application of a method.

Asking a question the evidence can answer

An instruction the evidence cannot answer as posed can often be reshaped into one it can, because the underlying commercial question is usually narrower than the question put to the examiner. Below is the translation we would work through with you on the scoping call, which is not charged for.

9

The commitment this page represents

We report what the evidence supports, including when it supports nothing, or supports the other side. The fee is the same. That is a worse outcome for us and a perfectly good one for you, and it is the reason this is worth commissioning rather than taking our word for it.

The rate is fixed before we look at anything and does not move with what we find, so nobody here has a reason to stretch a finding past its reach. The scoping call is not charged for, and neither is the call that ends with us saying the data cannot answer your question, or that you do not need a forensic examiner at all.
