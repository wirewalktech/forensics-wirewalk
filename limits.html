1

Why this is a published page and not a disclaimer

Every finding has a reach. Past that point the same artefact will support two or three explanations at once, and the artefact has nothing to say about which of them is true. So I do not treat the reach as a caveat bolted on at the end. It is part of the finding, and it is the part an opposing examiner will spend their time on.

The other reason to publish it is self-interested. If I have not written down the limits of a method before the engagement, I will be writing them for the first time under cross-examination, when the wording is no longer mine to choose. The same limits go into the report, attached as a clause to each finding, in this language. I will not take them out because a stronger letter is wanted.

There is also a question counsel asks early and rarely gets a straight answer to. If I pay for this, what will I actually have? Sometimes the answer is that the question as posed cannot be reached from the data at all, and that is much better known on the first call than after the invoice. Section 8 sets out how to turn one of those into a question the evidence can answer.

2

People, devices and accounts

The overstatements that do the most damage in this field are versions of one mistake, made at rising levels of ambition: reading a machine as a person, then an account as a person, then a mechanism as a motive.

2.1

A device is not a person

An examination recovers what the machine did. Which processes ran, which files were opened, written or deleted, which volumes were attached, and under which session or service context. All of that is a record about the machine. Getting from there to a named human takes material that lives off the device: an access-control or camera record, a second device the same person was carrying at the same time, a credential nobody else held, an admission. Where that material exists I can say whether the device record is consistent with it. Where it does not, the gap will not close by looking harder at the disk.

The competing explanations are ordinary ones. A shared workstation. An unlocked session left unattended. A remote support tool, a scheduled task running under a user context, malware operating with the interactive user's token. On the device alone, every one of those produces artefacts indistinguishable from the person you have in mind sitting down and typing.

Where a matter asks me for an attribution opinion, I state the methodology in writing before work starts, and name the non-device material it depends on.

2.2

An account action is not a person either

Authentication records show that a credential was presented and accepted. A credential is a bearer instrument. Multi-factor authentication raises the cost of a false attribution considerably, and it does not eliminate it: approval fatigue, a replayed session token, a helpdesk reset, a delegated mailbox and a shared administrative account all produce clean, unremarkable logs.

The useful work on a disputed account action is comparative. I build a baseline out of the account's own history, then state how far the disputed event departs from it. Source addresses, device identifiers, authentication method, usual hours, geography. That is a finding about consistency rather than identity, which is a real distinction and not a hedge. A consistent event is not proof, and an inconsistent one is not guilt.

2.3

Intent is a finding for the court

Artefacts record mechanism and sequence. They do not record a state of mind. I can often establish that material was destroyed, when, and by what means: a single deletion, a bulk removal, a wipe utility with a recognisable signature, a factory reset, a retention rule reaching its expiry. I can often also say whether a policy in force would have deleted the same material on the same day whether or not anybody wanted it gone.

This is the part instructing parties find hardest to accept. Where routine expiry and deliberate destruction are indistinguishable in the artefacts, I report that they cannot be separated, and the fact that a different answer would have suited the instructing party does not change it. Under Rule 37(e)(2) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure the severest measures for lost electronically stored information are available only on a finding that the party acted with the intent to deprive another party of the information's use in the litigation, a threshold that negligence, and even gross negligence, do not reach. That finding belongs to the court. What I supply is what existed, what was removed, when, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored from backups, shadow copies, journals or a second custodian.

3

Time, and the several clocks involved

Timestamps are the most persuasive thing in a forensic report and the most routinely misread. They persuade because they are precise. But precision and accuracy are different properties, and nothing in the record announces which one you are looking at.

3.1

There is never only one clock

A single event can involve the hardware clock, the operating system clock as disciplined by a time service between synchronisations, the clock of the application writing the record, and the clock of a server at the other end of the connection. Then whatever tool normalises all of that afterwards adds assumptions of its own. A workstation whose time was set wrong, or set back deliberately, writes the wrong time and nothing flags it.

Where a second record covering the same event was generated independently, I can test one clock against the other. Where there is no second record, the time is single-source, and I label it that way rather than let it stand as though something corroborated it.

3.2

Zones, epochs and conversions

NTFS stores times in UTC. FAT and exFAT store local time, so the same file copied between them changes its apparent time by the offset with nothing wrong at either end. Application databases store seconds since 1970, milliseconds since 1970, microseconds since 1601, or seconds since 2001, depending on what their designers chose. A tool that assumes the wrong one does not produce an obvious error. It produces a plausible wrong date, which is worse.

So I publish times in ISO 8601 with an explicit offset, in the form 2026-03-14T09:41:07-04:00, and state the conversion applied and the field it came from. A time printed without an offset should be treated as unverified, wherever you find it. That sounds fussy right up until an hour is the thing in dispute.

3.3

What filesystem times do and do not mean

"Created" does not mean authored. Copy a file and the creation time is the time of the copy, while the modification time is usually inherited from the original, which is why files routinely appear to have been modified before they existed. Sync clients, archive extraction, restores and indexing all move times about. Last-access updating is deferred or disabled by default on many Windows configurations, so an absent access time means very little.

On NTFS each file carries two sets of times, one in $STANDARD_INFORMATION and one in $FILE_NAME. Tools that alter timestamps have historically written the first and not the second, which is why disagreement between them is informative. Agreement proves nothing in return, because current tooling often writes both. Where time matters I go looking for an independent record of the event rather than trusting the file's account of itself.

4

Absence

Gaps in the record produce the most confident wrong conclusions in this field, and they go wrong in both directions at once. One reading treats a gap as proof that nothing happened. The other treats it as proof that something was hidden. Absence can be made to carry weight, but only on conditions, and the conditions are set out below.

4.1

Absence of an artefact is not evidence of absence

A thing that is not there may never have existed. It may have been removed by a person. It may equally have been removed by a retention job, a log rotation, a background trim cycle on solid-state storage, an upgrade that discarded an old database, or a reimage. Those are ordinary behaviours running on a schedule nobody thinks about, and several of them destroy data more thoroughly than a person trying to. I can separate the explanations only where some record survives that does the separating, and often enough that record is the missing thing itself. Where it is, the honest finding is that the question cannot be resolved from this material, and I write it that way rather than present silence as a result.

4.2

What a missing artefact can mean

There is a narrower positive result available here, and it is worth asking for by name. Where a system reliably creates a particular artefact for a class of event, and that artefact is present for comparable events in the same period but absent for the one in dispute, the absence starts to carry weight. Making that argument means stating how consistently the artefact is created, how long it survives, whether it can be suppressed and by whom, and how large the comparison set is. I state all four, because a bare assertion that "there would have been a record" does not survive contact with an opposing examiner.

The other honest use of absence is to mark the boundary of the record. Nothing predates the earliest surviving log. Where the retention window opens after the events of interest, the answer to "did this happen" is "the record does not reach that far back". That is not the same answer as "no", and I will not report it as though it were.

5

Particular artefacts and how far they reach

These are the artefacts that get asked about by name, and the ones opposing reports lean on hardest. Each is genuinely useful. Each also reaches less far than its reputation.

5.1

USB records, and evidence of copying

Windows records removable devices attached to a machine. The USBSTOR keys hold a vendor string, a product string and an instance identifier; connection times come from associated keys and from setupapi.dev.log, and a per-user MountPoints2 key ties a profile to a volume. Taken together that is good evidence that a device reporting a particular identity was presented to that machine, and often when.

What they do not record is a copy. Not in either direction, and not how much. They also do not identify the physical device with certainty: the serial string is supplied by the device itself, inexpensive controllers repeat them across whole production runs, and where the second character of the instance identifier is an ampersand, Windows generated the string precisely because the device supplied none. And they say nothing about who attached it. Evidence of copying comes from other artefacts with limits of their own, and how much data was written to a removable device is generally not recoverable from the host at all.

Where a copy is recorded, what is recorded is the operation and not the reason for it. A file arriving in a personal cloud folder, on an external disk or in a second account looks identical whether someone put it there deliberately or a sync client, a backup agent, a roaming profile or a mail rule did what it was configured to do months earlier. Telling those apart takes the configuration that was in force at the time, and a pattern across many files and dates rather than one copy event. Where the configuration cannot be established from the material, the two explanations are not separable, and that is what I report.

5.2

Deleted data, and why recovery is conditional

Whether deleted data comes back is decided by the medium, not by how hard anyone tries. On a conventional hard disk, deleting a file commonly unlinks it while the content stays in unallocated space until something reuses it, and recovery there is routine. Solid-state storage behaves differently. The controller is told the blocks are free and erases them on its own schedule, independently of the operating system, so by the time a drive reaches me the content is frequently gone at the hardware level and no tool, budget or effort returns it. Full-disk encryption closes the door another way, since unallocated space is then ciphertext with no structure to carve.

The same thing happens a level up, inside applications. Messages and browser history live in databases that mark a deleted row free and keep it until the space is reused, or until a vacuum operation rewrites the file. Several applications run that automatically, with no user involvement. Often the better prospect is not the medium the file was deleted from at all: it is a backup, a shadow copy, a synchronised second device, or the counterparty's own copy of the same message.

Before you pay for an attempt I set out what the medium and its encryption state permit, and which other source is the better prospect. That is a statement about conditions and not a prediction of the result. An attempt that recovers nothing bills exactly as one that recovers everything.

5.3

Cloud logs record access, not content

A cloud audit record says that an operation of a stated type was performed against a named object at a stated time by a named principal. It does not contain the message, the document, or the version as it stood at that moment. Whether the content can be produced is a question about retention, versioning, licence tier and whether a hold was in place. The log does not answer it.

Two gaps here are common enough to be worth checking in your own tenant today rather than during an incident, against your own licence and your provider's current documentation, because both move. In Microsoft 365, MailItemsAccessed is the record showing which messages were actually accessed, which makes it the record that governs what has to be notified. It depends on audit licence tier and on the audit configuration in force at the time, and it is absent from many tenants that assume they have it. In AWS, CloudTrail data events record object-level access; they are not enabled by default and are billed separately when they are. Enabling either during an incident logs forward only and recovers nothing. Audit pipelines also throttle and truncate under load, so a quiet period in a log can turn out to be a quiet period in the pipeline.

5.4

Encrypted, partial and defended data

An encrypted volume with no key and no recovery credential is not going to open, and the money is better spent locating the key. Mobile and cloud acquisitions are logical and partial by nature. What can be obtained depends on the handset, the operating system version, the state the device was in when it was seized, and the tooling available that month, and it moves in both directions over time. A result achieved on one device last year is not a promise about this one, which is why deeper mobile extraction is quoted only after the handset has been inspected.

Anti-forensic tooling exists, it is freely available, and it sometimes works. Its use sometimes leaves recognisable traces and sometimes leaves none. Where I find traces I report them as what they are. Where I find none, I do not offer that as proof that nothing was done.

6

Automated output is not a finding

Forensic suites present interpretations as facts. A column headed "USB inserted" reads better than a column headed "this tool's current parse of a partly documented structure". Different tools parse the same structure differently, behaviour changes between versions, and two reputable products disagreeing about one artefact is unremarkable. That disagreement is itself information, and the tool version is part of the finding.

For any finding that matters I read behind the output to the underlying structure, record the tool and version, and state what I examined directly rather than accepted from a report pane. Carved data needs particular care. It arrives with no filename, no path and no filesystem timestamp, carrying only what the file itself contains, and a carved fragment tabulated beside ordinary files implies a context it does not have. Rule 702(d) of the Federal Rules of Evidence requires that the opinion reflects a reliable application of the principles and methods to the facts of the case. An output nobody has read behind is not an application of a method.

8

Asking a question the evidence can answer

An instruction the evidence cannot answer as posed can often be reshaped into one it can, because the commercial question underneath is usually narrower than the question that reaches the examiner. This is the translation I would work through with you on the scoping call, which is not charged for.

9

The commitment this page represents

I report what the evidence supports, including when it supports nothing, or supports the other side. The fee is the same either way. That is a worse outcome for me and a perfectly good one for you, and it is the reason this is worth commissioning rather than taking my word for it.

The rate is fixed before I look at anything and it does not move with what I find, so I have no reason to stretch a finding past its reach. The scoping call is not charged for. Neither is the call that ends with me saying the data cannot answer your question, or that you do not need a forensic examiner at all.
