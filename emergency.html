2

Containment and preservation are two different jobs

Almost every avoidable loss in the first hour comes from doing containment in a way that also does destruction. Someone stops the bleeding and wipes the record in the same motion, with the best of intentions. Everything below is a way of containing that keeps the record.

You do not need to have decided whether to instruct anyone, or who. The six steps in section 3 are the same whichever examiner you end up calling. They are also the same if you never call one.

3

The six things to do first, in this order

  1. Do not re-image, rebuild, “clean” or restore over an affected system. This is the most complete destruction of evidence available to you in the first hour. It is almost never done by anyone careless. It is done by good IT staff doing exactly the job they were trained to do, quickly, under pressure, before anyone has said the word evidence out loud. If a system has to be rebuilt to keep the business running, image it first. Or rebuild onto new hardware and set the original aside, powered down and labelled.
  2. Do not reboot. Isolate instead. Unplug the network cable, shut the switch port, or use your EDR’s host-isolation feature. If your product separates isolation from automatic remediation, switch the remediation off. Leave the machine powered on and unlocked. Power it off only if you cannot isolate it and cannot otherwise stop the spread. Memory is gone the moment the power is, and it is being overwritten the whole time the machine runs, so the only way to keep any of it is to capture it. Before you power off an encrypted machine, check that the recovery key is escrowed somewhere you can reach. A running, unlocked BitLocker or FileVault machine is readable. A powered-off one with no key is a block of ciphertext, and nobody can do anything with it.
  3. Do not reflexively pull a domain controller, storage head, industrial controller or clinical device off the network. On some systems, isolating in the wrong order does more harm than the intrusion did. Take the order from someone who knows what depends on what. Then write down what was decided and why, even if it is one line in a notebook.
  4. Suspend backup retention, expiry, garbage collection and replication now. Do not mount or restore from backups until someone has established whether the backup system itself is compromised. A routine retention cycle can delete your last clean restore point tonight. Replication can overwrite good copies with encrypted ones. Mounting an infected backup on a clean network puts the attacker back inside. One thing people do not expect: the backup console’s own logs are often the best evidence there is of how long the attacker was in.
  5. Preserve the logs that expire, today. Raise local log sizes first, because it costs nothing and buys days. Then export the relevant window out of the affected tenant, because an attacker with administrative access can turn logging off going forward. The figures that follow are as published by Microsoft and current at the date on this page, and they are worth checking against your own tenant and licence. Microsoft 365 Audit (Standard) retention is 180 days by default for records generated since 17 October 2023. MailItemsAccessed, the record showing which messages were actually read and therefore what actually has to be notified about, is a Premium feature and does not exist in lower-licensed tenants. Exchange mailbox audit entries default to 90 days. Entra ID sign-in logs are 7 days on the free tier and 30 days with P1 or P2 unless you export them. Turning auditing on now logs forward only. It recovers nothing.
  6. Start a log at minute one. UTC timestamps, who did what, on which system, and why. Include the reboots. Include the mistakes people made before anyone was thinking about evidence. It is the first thing I would ask you for and the first thing opposing counsel will ask for, and an honest log is worth far more to you than a tidy one. Move the conversation off the email and chat you think may be compromised.

4

The four situations, and what each one needs first

Find yours and do the numbered steps. If two of them apply, do both. A fraudulent wire and a departing employee are very often the same matter seen from two directions.

4.1

Ransomware

  1. Isolate in one coordinated sweep, not one machine at a time across an afternoon.
  2. Preserve the note, its filename, and a sample of encrypted files with their extensions. That is often what identifies the family.
  3. Keep one fully encrypted machine untouched as the reference.
  4. Check whether patching, imaging, backup expiry or log rotation is scheduled for tonight, and suspend it.
  5. Do not delete the note. Do not rename or “try” encrypted files. Do not run a full anti-virus clean, and do not plug a technician’s own USB drive into anything.

I do not advise on whether to pay, I do not negotiate, and I will not make contact with a threat actor. That question runs through sanctions screening, your carrier’s position and law enforcement, and it belongs with counsel rather than with me.

4.2

Fraudulent wire, or a compromised mailbox

  1. Call the originating bank’s fraud desk by phone, within the hour, and ask for a recall or a hold. Call the receiving bank too if you can identify it. Do this before anything else on this list: the money clock is shorter than the forensic clock.
  2. File at ic3.gov immediately, so that the FBI’s Recovery Asset Team can consider the Financial Fraud Kill Chain. Do not work out first whether you qualify. IC3 applies its own conditions and they change.
  3. Capture the inbox and transport rules before you delete any of them, and look for the rule with a blank name or a single-character name. Capture forwarding addresses at both mailbox and tenant level, delegate permissions, registered MFA methods, OAuth consents and registered devices. Deleting a rule also deletes the evidence that the rule was ever there.
  4. Revoke sessions and tokens, remove attacker-registered MFA methods and app passwords, and then reset the password, in that order. A password reset on its own does not evict an attacker holding a valid refresh token.
  5. Sweep every mailbox, not only the one somebody noticed.
  6. Do not delete the fraudulent emails. Preserve full headers, not forwarded copies.
  7. Tell accounts payable to verify every pending payment-detail change by calling back on a number you already held, never on one taken from the email.
4.3

Departing employee

  1. Get the laptop out of the return-and-reimage queue before you do anything else. Physically out, labelled and logged.
  2. Do not boot it. And do not let anyone have a quick look, however well meant. Booting alters thousands of timestamps, and it can trigger a cloud sync that deletes the copied files, or an MDM wipe. Write down the state you found it in.
  3. Put the same hold on the mailbox, the cloud drive and the home directory. Offboarding scripts delete all three on a timer.
  4. Never log into the individual’s personal email, personal cloud account or personal device, even from a saved session on the company laptop, and even if they used it for work. It is also the hardest one to follow, because the session is often sitting right there, already logged in. It carries real legal exposure and it can taint everything else you do.
  5. Do not tip off the subject, and recognise what does the tipping: an abrupt account disable, a device recall, a new DLP block, a conspicuous meeting.

When and how the individual is told depends on the employment agreement and on applicable law. That is counsel’s decision, not IT’s.

4.4

Lost or stolen device

  1. Document the encryption state from the management console before you send the wipe. Once the device is wiped and drops out of the console, the record that it was encrypted may no longer exist anywhere. That record is often the whole difference between a notifiable event and a documented non-event.
  2. Record the identifiers, the last check-in time, the address and location it reported, its compliance state, and what was stored locally as against what was only reachable through a session.
  3. Revoke sessions, tokens and device trust. That contains the account risk without destroying the record.
  4. Record whether the wipe was queued, acknowledged or completed. A wipe command only runs when the device next checks in, so treat an unacknowledged wipe as a device still at large.
  5. File the police report and keep the report number.

If the device is under a litigation hold the analysis changes entirely, and nothing should be wiped until counsel says so.

Call your insurer or broker before you engage anyone

Policies commonly require notice within a short window measured from discovery. They commonly require the carrier’s prior consent as well, or the use of panel counsel and panel vendors. Hire outside that and you can reduce or void cover you have already paid for.

That applies to me as much as to anyone else. I am not on a carrier panel, so if your policy requires a panel vendor, instructing me may be the thing that costs you the cover. It is why this section sits above the one about my fee.

Containment does not wait for the carrier. Procurement does. Do section 3 now and make the notification call alongside it. Many carriers run a hotline that reaches counsel faster than your own organisation can.

What I will not do, including at 02:00

  1. Take a fee that depends on what I find, on the outcome of the matter, or on which side retains me. The rate is the same either way and it is set before I look at anything.
  2. Take or pay a referral fee, in either direction. Not to counsel, not to brokers, not to insurers, not to anyone else who sends me work.
  3. Take a commission from any hardware, software, platform or hosting vendor I place, or sell you the remediation I was paid to investigate.
  4. Advise on whether to pay a ransom, negotiate with a threat actor, or make contact with one.
  5. Touch a device, mailbox or account without documented authority from someone entitled to give it, whatever the account holder’s relationship to you and whatever the story about ownership.
  6. Opine on who was at the keyboard from device evidence alone. A device is not a person.
  7. Opine that a party acted with the intent to destroy evidence. I report what the artefacts show: what existed, what was deleted, when, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored from elsewhere. Intent is a finding for the court.
  8. Take a matter I cannot finish inside your deadline. I will say so on the first call rather than accept it and miss it.
  9. Take a matter that requires a certification, a licence or a qualification I do not hold. I identify that at scoping, name it in the engagement letter, and either bring in someone who holds it or decline.
  10. Promise you a finding, a recovered file, an unlocked handset, or that anything I produce will be admitted. Admissibility is the court’s decision and nobody can guarantee it.
