2

Containment and preservation are two different jobs

In the first hour, containment and preservation are two different jobs. Almost every avoidable loss comes from doing containment in a way that also does destruction. Everything below is a way of containing that happens to keep the record.

You do not need to have decided whether to instruct anyone, or whom. The six steps in section 3 are the same whichever examiner you eventually call, and they are the same if you never call one.

3

The six things to do first, in this order

  1. Do not re-image, rebuild, “clean” or restore over an affected system. This is the most complete destruction of evidence available in the first hour, and it is almost always done by competent IT staff doing the job they were trained to do. If a system must be rebuilt to keep the business running, image it first, or rebuild onto new hardware and set the original aside, powered down and labelled.
  2. Do not reboot. Isolate instead. Unplug the network cable, shut the switch port, or use your EDR’s host-isolation feature — with automatic remediation switched off, if your product separates the two — and leave the machine powered and unlocked. Power off only if isolation is impossible and spread cannot otherwise be stopped. Memory does not survive a power-off and is overwritten continuously while the machine runs, so the only thing that preserves it is capturing it. Before powering off any encrypted machine, confirm the recovery key is escrowed: a running, unlocked BitLocker or FileVault machine is readable, and a powered-off one without a key is a block of ciphertext.
  3. Do not pull a domain controller, storage head, industrial controller or clinical device off the network reflexively. Isolation order is a decision, not a reflex, and on some systems it does more harm than the intrusion. Take the order from someone who knows what depends on what, and write down what was decided and why.
  4. Suspend backup retention, expiry, garbage collection and replication now. Do not mount or restore from backups until someone has established whether the backup system itself is compromised. A routine retention cycle can delete your last clean restore point tonight; replication can overwrite good copies with encrypted ones; mounting an infected backup on a clean network reintroduces the attacker. The backup console’s own logs are often the best evidence of how long the attacker was inside.
  5. Preserve the logs that expire, today. Raise local log sizes — it costs nothing and buys days. Export the relevant window out of the affected tenant, because an attacker with administrative access can turn logging off going forward. Figures as published by Microsoft and current at the date on this page, and worth checking for your own tenant and licence: Microsoft 365 Audit (Standard) retention is 180 days by default for records generated since 17 October 2023; MailItemsAccessed — the record showing which messages were actually read, and therefore what actually has to be notified about — is a Premium feature that does not exist in lower-licensed tenants; Exchange mailbox audit entries default to 90 days; Entra ID sign-in logs are 7 days on the free tier and 30 days with P1 or P2 unless exported. Turning auditing on now logs forward only. It recovers nothing.
  6. Start a log at minute one. UTC timestamps, who did what, on which system, and why — including the reboots and the mistakes made before anyone thought about evidence. It is the first thing any examiner or opposing counsel asks for, and the honest version is worth far more than a tidy one. Move the conversation off the possibly-compromised email and chat.

4

The four situations, and what each one needs first

Find yours and do the numbered steps. If two apply, do both — a fraudulent wire and a departing employee are frequently the same matter seen from two directions.

4.1

Ransomware

  1. Isolate in a coordinated sweep, not one machine at a time over an afternoon.
  2. Preserve the note, its filename, and a sample of encrypted files with their extensions. That is often what identifies the family.
  3. Keep one fully encrypted machine untouched as the reference.
  4. Check whether patching, imaging, backup expiry or log rotation is scheduled tonight, and suspend it.
  5. Do not delete the note, do not rename or “try” encrypted files, do not run a full anti-virus clean, and do not connect a technician’s own USB drive to anything.

We do not advise on whether to pay, we do not negotiate, and we do not contact a threat actor. Any payment analysis involves sanctions screening, your carrier’s position and law enforcement, and it belongs with counsel.

4.2

Fraudulent wire, or a compromised mailbox

  1. The money clock is shorter than the forensic clock. Call the originating bank’s fraud desk by phone within the hour and ask for a recall or a hold. Call the receiving bank as well if you can identify it.
  2. File at ic3.gov immediately, so that the FBI’s Recovery Asset Team can consider the Financial Fraud Kill Chain. File rather than pre-judge whether you qualify: IC3 applies its own conditions and they change.
  3. Capture the inbox and transport rules — including the blank- or single-character-named one — forwarding addresses at both mailbox and tenant level, delegate permissions, registered MFA methods, OAuth consents and registered devices, before you delete any of them. Deleting the rule destroys the record of the rule.
  4. Revoke sessions and tokens, remove attacker-registered MFA methods and app passwords, and then reset the password. A password reset alone does not evict an attacker holding a valid refresh token.
  5. Sweep every mailbox, not only the one that was noticed.
  6. Do not delete the fraudulent emails. Preserve full headers, not forwarded copies.
  7. Tell accounts payable to verify every pending payment-detail change by callback to a number you already held, not to one in the email.
4.3

Departing employee

  1. Take the laptop out of the return-and-reimage queue before anything else — physically, labelled, logged.
  2. Do not boot it, and do not let IT have a quick look. Booting alters thousands of timestamps, can trigger a cloud sync that deletes the copied files, and can trigger an MDM wipe. Document the state you found it in.
  3. Put the same hold on the mailbox, the cloud drive and the home directory. Offboarding scripts delete all three on a timer.
  4. Never log into the individual’s personal email, personal cloud account or personal device, even from a saved session on the company laptop, and even if they used it for work. It carries real legal exposure and it can taint everything else.
  5. Do not tip off the subject, and recognise what does the tipping: an abrupt account disable, a device recall, a new DLP block, a conspicuous meeting.

When and how the individual is told depends on the employment agreement and on applicable law. That is counsel’s decision, not IT’s.

4.4

Lost or stolen device

  1. Document the encryption state from the management console before you send the wipe. Once the device is wiped and drops out of the console, the record that it was encrypted may no longer be available anywhere, and that record is often the difference between a notifiable event and a documented non-event.
  2. Record the identifiers, the last check-in time, the address and location it reported, its compliance state, and what was stored locally as against what was only reachable through a session.
  3. Revoke sessions, tokens and device trust. That contains the account risk without destroying the record.
  4. Record whether the wipe was queued, acknowledged or completed. A wipe command runs only when the device checks in, so treat an unacknowledged wipe as a device still at large.
  5. File the police report and keep the report number.

If the device is under a litigation hold the analysis changes entirely, and nothing should be wiped until counsel says so.

5

Call your insurer or broker before you engage anyone

Policies commonly require notice within a short window measured from discovery, and commonly require the carrier’s prior consent, or the use of panel counsel and panel vendors. Hiring outside that can reduce or void cover you have already paid for. This is true of us as much as of anyone else, and it is the reason this section sits above the one about our fee. We are not on a carrier panel, so if your policy requires a panel vendor, instructing us may be the thing that costs you the cover.

Containment does not wait for the carrier; procurement does. Do section 3 now and make the notification call in parallel. Many carriers run a hotline that reaches counsel faster than your own organisation can.

What we will not do, including at 02:00

  1. Take a fee that depends on what we find, on the outcome of the matter, or on which side retains us. The rate is the same either way and it is set before we look at anything.
  2. Take or pay a referral fee, in either direction — to counsel, brokers, insurers, or anyone else who sends us work.
  3. Take a commission from any hardware, software, platform or hosting vendor we place, or sell you the remediation we were paid to investigate.
  4. Advise on whether to pay a ransom, negotiate with a threat actor, or make contact with one.
  5. Touch a device, mailbox or account without documented authority from someone entitled to give it, whatever the account holder’s relationship to you and whatever the story about ownership.
  6. Opine on who was at the keyboard from device evidence alone. A device is not a person.
  7. Opine that a party acted with the intent to destroy evidence. We report what the artefacts show — what existed, what was deleted, when, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored from elsewhere. Intent is a finding for the court.
  8. Take a matter we cannot finish inside your deadline. We will say so on the first call rather than accept it and miss it.
  9. Take a matter that requires a certification, a licence or a qualification we do not hold. We identify that at scoping, name it in the engagement letter, and either bring in someone who holds it or decline.
  10. Promise you a finding, a recovered file, an unlocked handset, or that anything we produce will be admitted. Admissibility is the court’s decision and no one can guarantee it.
