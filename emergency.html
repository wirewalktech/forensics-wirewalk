If you do nothing else here, do the six steps below. They are the same whichever examiner you end up calling, and the same if you never call one. Most avoidable loss in the first hour comes from containing and destroying in the same motion.

Six things to do first, in this order

  1. Do not re-image, rebuild, “clean” or restore over an affected system. This is the most complete destruction of evidence available to you tonight, and it is almost always done by good IT staff doing the job they were trained to do, fast, before anyone says the word evidence. If a system must be rebuilt to keep trading, image it first — or rebuild onto new hardware and set the original aside, powered down and labelled.
  2. Do not reboot. Isolate instead. Pull the network cable, shut the switch port, or use your EDR’s host isolation. If the product couples isolation to automatic remediation, switch the remediation off. Leave the machine powered on and unlocked. Power off only if you cannot isolate and cannot otherwise stop the spread. Memory dies with the power. Before powering off an encrypted machine, check the recovery key is escrowed somewhere you can reach: a running unlocked BitLocker or FileVault machine is readable, a powered-off one with no key is a block of ciphertext.
  3. Do not reflexively pull a domain controller, storage head, industrial controller or clinical device off the network. Isolating in the wrong order can do more harm than the intrusion. Take the order from someone who knows what depends on what, then write down what was decided and why — one line in a notebook will do.
  4. Suspend backup retention, expiry, garbage collection and replication now. A routine retention cycle can delete your last clean restore point tonight, and replication can overwrite good copies with encrypted ones. Do not mount or restore from backups until someone has established whether the backup system itself is compromised — mounting an infected backup on a clean network puts the attacker back inside. The backup console’s own logs are often the best evidence of how long the attacker was in.
  5. Preserve the logs that expire, today. Raise local log sizes first: it costs nothing and buys days. Then export the relevant window out of the affected tenant, because an attacker with administrative access can turn logging off going forward. Defaults below. Turning auditing on tonight logs forward only; it recovers nothing.
  6. Start a log at minute one. UTC timestamps, who did what, on which system, and why. Include the reboots. Include the mistakes made before anyone was thinking about evidence — an honest log is worth far more to you than a tidy one. Move the conversation off the email and chat you think may be compromised.

Then find your situation

If two apply, do both. A fraudulent wire and a departing employee are very often the same matter seen from two directions.

Ransomware

  1. Isolate in one coordinated sweep, not one machine at a time across an afternoon.
  2. Preserve the note, its filename, and a sample of encrypted files with their extensions. That is often what identifies the family.
  3. Keep one fully encrypted machine untouched as the reference.
  4. Suspend anything scheduled for tonight: patching, imaging, backup expiry, log rotation.
  5. Do not delete the note, rename or “try” encrypted files, run a full anti-virus clean, or plug a technician’s own USB drive into anything.

I do not advise on whether to pay, do not negotiate, and will not contact a threat actor. That question runs through sanctions screening, your carrier and law enforcement, and belongs with counsel.

Fraudulent wire, or a compromised mailbox

  1. Call the originating bank’s fraud desk by phone, within the hour, and ask for a recall or hold. Call the receiving bank too if you can identify it. Do this first: the money clock is shorter than the forensic clock.
  2. File at ic3.gov immediately, so the FBI’s Recovery Asset Team can consider the Financial Fraud Kill Chain. Do not work out first whether you qualify; IC3 applies its own conditions and they change.
  3. Capture inbox and transport rules before deleting any of them, and look for the rule with a blank or single-character name. Capture forwarding addresses at mailbox and tenant level, delegate permissions, registered MFA methods, OAuth consents and registered devices. Deleting a rule deletes the evidence it existed.
  4. Revoke sessions and tokens, remove attacker-registered MFA methods and app passwords, then reset the password — in that order. A password reset alone does not evict an attacker holding a valid refresh token.
  5. Sweep every mailbox, not only the one somebody noticed.
  6. Do not delete the fraudulent emails. Preserve full headers, not forwarded copies.
  7. Tell accounts payable to verify every pending payment-detail change by calling back on a number you already held, never one taken from the email.

Departing employee

  1. Get the laptop out of the return-and-reimage queue first. Physically out, labelled, logged.
  2. Do not boot it, and do not let anyone have a quick look. Booting alters thousands of timestamps and can trigger a cloud sync that deletes the copied files, or an MDM wipe. Write down the state you found it in.
  3. Hold the mailbox, the cloud drive and the home directory. Offboarding scripts delete all three on a timer.
  4. Never log into the individual’s personal email, cloud account or device — even from a session already open on the company laptop, and even if they used it for work. It carries real legal exposure and can taint everything else you do.
  5. Do not tip off the subject, and recognise what does the tipping: an abrupt account disable, a device recall, a new DLP block, a conspicuous meeting.

When and how the individual is told depends on the employment agreement and applicable law. That is counsel’s decision, not IT’s.

Lost or stolen device

  1. Document the encryption state from the management console before you send the wipe. Once the device drops out of the console, the record that it was encrypted may not exist anywhere — and that record is often the difference between a notifiable event and a documented non-event.
  2. Record the identifiers, last check-in time, reported address and location, compliance state, and what was stored locally as against reachable only through a session.
  3. Revoke sessions, tokens and device trust. That contains the account risk without destroying the record.
  4. Record whether the wipe was queued, acknowledged or completed. A wipe runs only when the device next checks in, so treat an unacknowledged wipe as a device still at large.
  5. File the police report and keep the report number.

Under a litigation hold the analysis changes entirely, and nothing is wiped until counsel says so.
