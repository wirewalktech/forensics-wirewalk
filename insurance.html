1

Call the carrier or the broker before you engage anyone

If your organisation carries cyber, technology errors and omissions, crime, or management liability cover, that policy almost certainly has something to say about two things: when you must tell the carrier, and whom you are permitted to hire at the carrier's expense. Both of those questions are live from the moment you discover an incident, and both are ordinarily settled before a forensic examiner is instructed — including before we are instructed.

This is the first section on the page because it is the recommendation that costs us work. An insured who calls the carrier first may be directed to a panel vendor who is not us. We would rather lose that engagement than be the reason a claim is reduced.

1.1

Notice is usually measured from discovery, not from the day it looked serious

Policies commonly require notice of an incident, a circumstance or a claim within a short window, and the window commonly runs from the point of discovery rather than from the point at which the organisation concluded the matter was worth reporting. The practical effect is that the clock can already have been running for two days by the time anyone reads the policy, because the first two days were spent establishing what had happened.

Where notice is written as a condition, late notice can give the carrier an argument it would not otherwise have had, on a loss that is squarely within the cover. We are not telling you what your policy says. We are telling you that the question is time-sensitive and that nobody inside the first hour of an incident is thinking about it.

1.2

Consent to expenses and the approved-vendor condition

Separately from notice, many policies condition the payment of incident-response costs on the carrier's prior written consent, and many operate a panel: approved breach counsel, approved forensic vendors, sometimes approved notification providers. The wordings differ, and the differences are the whole question. Some allow a non-panel vendor with consent that is not to be unreasonably withheld. Some pay a non-panel vendor only at the negotiated panel rate. Some exclude costs incurred before consent altogether.

Hiring outside that structure can reduce or void cover you have already paid for. The exposure is not the size of the examiner's invoice; the exposure is the cover that invoice was supposed to sit inside.

1.3

Containment does not wait for the carrier. Procurement does.

Nothing on this page should delay the first hour. Isolating a host, suspending backup expiry and replication, raising log sizes, exporting the audit window out of a tenant, taking a returned laptop out of the reimage queue — none of that requires the carrier's approval, and none of it should wait for a call to be returned. Isolation order is still a decision rather than a reflex on a domain controller, a storage head, an industrial controller or a clinical device. Those steps, and that caveat, are set out at /emergency, written to be done by your own staff.

What should wait is signature. The engagement letter, the purchase order, the authorisation to spend: that is procurement, and procurement is the part the policy governs. The two jobs are separable, and the common failure is to treat them as one and do neither.

1.4

The hotline usually reaches counsel faster than your own escalation chain

Many carriers and brokers run a staffed incident line that routes an insured to breach counsel quickly, commonly outside business hours — often faster than an organisation's own escalation path produces its own general counsel on a Sunday evening. Getting counsel in first is what makes it possible to structure the forensic work to support a privilege claim. It does not make the work privileged: forensic reports are not automatically work product, courts have ordered them produced, and we can structure our work to support a claim without promising that one holds. If you do not know the number, your broker does, and the hours it is actually staffed are worth asking about at the same time; it is ordinarily printed on the policy schedule.

Making a notification does not commit you to pursuing a claim. Whether a notification of circumstance carries any consequence at renewal is a question for your broker, and it is a fair one to ask them in the same call.

2

What to put in front of the broker

These are the provisions that decide who pays for a forensic examination and on what terms. We describe them in general because policy wordings vary widely and we will not characterise any particular carrier's requirements. Read your own policy, or have the broker read it with you.

Provisions that govern instructing an examiner
ProvisionWhat to establishWhat this does not establish
Notice The window, what event starts it, whom notice must be given to, and in what form. That giving notice preserves cover. A late-notice argument turns on the wording and the facts, and notice given correctly settles only the notice question.
Consent to expenses Whether costs require prior written consent, and whether anything incurred before consent is recoverable at all. That consent to one vendor's costs extends to another's, to a later phase of the same matter, or to a ceiling increase.
Panel counsel and approved vendors Whether the panel is mandatory or default, and what the route is for using someone off it. That a firm's presence on a panel is a statement about the quality of its work, or that its absence is a statement about ours.
Retention or deductible The amount, and whether forensic cost sits inside it. What the eventual total will be. The retention is a threshold, not an estimate of the work.
Sublimits Whether incident response, forensics or notification carries its own sublimit below the policy limit. That work stops when a sublimit is exhausted. A sublimit caps what is reimbursed, not what is examined; whether the work continues past it is your authorisation decision, taken against the written ceiling, and not the carrier's.
Rate treatment off panel Whether a non-panel vendor is paid at its own rate, at panel rates, or not at all. What our rate would be. Our rate is published at /rates and does not change according to who is paying it.
Definitions How the policy defines incident, claim, and the trigger — discovery, first suspicion, or determination. Which definition your facts fall under. That is a coverage question for counsel and the broker, and we do not answer it.

Ask the broker one closing question: if you instruct a non-panel examiner today, what specifically happens to the claim. It is knowable in advance, and it is much harder to obtain once the invoices exist.

3

Our panel position, stated plainly

We are not on any carrier's approved-vendor panel. We do not hold a pre-approval, a framework agreement, or any status with a carrier or a broker that would cause your consent question to be answered in advance.

We state that here, on our own site, in the plainest available wording, because the alternative is an insured discovering it after the work has been done. A practice that leaves the question ambiguous is transferring a coverage risk onto its client in order to win the engagement, and that is not a trade we will make.

A matter under a retention can still produce a notification obligation, a termination, or litigation, and it is scoped on that basis rather than on the invoice.
3.2

What we will and will not do with your carrier

If the carrier or breach counsel wants to assess us, we answer their questions directly and supply whatever they reasonably need — the method document at /method, the published rate schedule, our conflicts position. If they decline, that is their decision: we will not lobby them, go around them to the insured, or press you to instruct us anyway. If a panel firm is instructed instead and wants a defined piece of the work done by us, we are available for that.

Where the engagement is arranged in advance rather than during an incident, the consent and conflicts questions can be settled while nothing is on fire. That is what the standby agreement at /standby is for, and a pre-cleared vendor position is one of the things it is worth doing with your broker in the room.

4

What we will not do where an insurer is involved

We will not

  1. Take or pay a referral fee, in either direction, to a broker, a carrier, breach counsel, or anyone else who sends us work. There is no arrangement of any kind between this practice and any insurer or intermediary.
  2. Tell you that your loss is covered, that your notice is timely, that a cost will be reimbursed, or that an incident meets a policy definition. Those are coverage questions and they belong with counsel and the broker.
  3. Advise you to delay a notification, or to describe an incident in a particular way so that it falls inside or outside a definition. What we write about an incident is what the artefacts support, and it is written knowing a carrier may read it.
  4. Represent ourselves to a carrier, a broker or an insured as panel-approved, pre-approved, pre-qualified or otherwise holding a status we do not hold.
  5. Vary our rate according to whether an insurer, a law firm or an individual is paying it. The schedule at /rates is the schedule, and a matter with a carrier behind it is not priced differently from one without.
  6. Bill a carrier directly on the footing that the retaining party is off the hook if the carrier declines. Our client is the party that signs the engagement letter; who reimburses that party is between them and their insurer, and we say so before the work starts rather than in a collections letter afterwards.
5

Our own insurance position, and how to ask about it

Vendor onboarding at a law firm or a corporate insured commonly requires evidence of professional liability and cyber cover at stated limits, sometimes with additional-insured or waiver-of-subrogation wording, and evidenced by a certificate issued by the carrier rather than by an assertion from the vendor.

We do not publish our position on this page. A sentence on a website is not evidence of cover; a certificate from the carrier is, and it is the only form of the answer that is worth anything to your procurement team. Put the question to us directly before you engage us and we will answer it in writing: whether cover is in place, at what limits, what form of evidence is available and from whom, and any onboarding requirement we cannot meet. Where our position does not satisfy your requirement, we will tell you that instead of sending you a document that does not.

The same discipline applies to every other condition. Where an engagement requires a certified examiner, an accredited laboratory, or a licence in your venue, that condition is identified at scoping, named in the engagement letter, and either satisfied before work begins or the matter is declined. We check the licensing position for your venue and either hold it, work under one, or decline the matter. We do not publish a state-by-state table; the map is contested and it moves.

6

If the carrier directs you elsewhere

Then go. An insured who follows the policy and instructs the panel vendor has made the correct decision, and it remains the correct decision even where we would have been glad of the work. If the panel firm turns out to be the wrong size for the matter, or the insured later wants a second examiner to look at the same artefacts, that conversation can be had at that point, with the consent question settled first.

If we are already on the phone when you discover the panel condition, we stop. The scoping call is free, we do not charge for the call that ends with us telling you to speak to your carrier, and nothing said on it creates an engagement. Where a preliminary approach has been logged for conflicts purposes, the logging creates no engagement either, and what was said on the call stays confidential whether or not you ever instruct us.

7

This is not coverage advice

Nothing on this page is legal advice, coverage advice, or advice about your particular policy, and it does not create any relationship between us. We are not brokers, we are not adjusters, and we do not act for insurers. Policy wordings differ from carrier to carrier and from year to year within the same programme, and a general description of a common condition is not a description of yours. The only authority on what your policy requires is your policy, read with your broker and your counsel.

The whole of the recommendation here is one sentence: make the call to the carrier or the broker before you instruct anyone, and make it in parallel with containment rather than after it.
