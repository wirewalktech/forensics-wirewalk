1

Call the carrier or the broker before you engage anyone

If you carry cyber cover, or technology errors and omissions, or crime, or management liability, that policy almost certainly has a view on two things. When you have to tell the carrier, and whom you are allowed to hire at the carrier's expense. Both questions are live from the moment you discover an incident, and both are ordinarily settled before a forensic examiner is instructed. That includes before I am instructed.

I have put this first because it is the recommendation that costs me work. An insured who calls the carrier first may well be pointed at a panel vendor who is not me. I would rather lose that engagement than be the reason a claim gets reduced.

1.1

Notice is usually measured from discovery, not from the day it looked serious

Policies commonly require notice of an incident, a circumstance or a claim within a short window, and that window commonly runs from the point of discovery rather than from the point at which somebody concluded the thing was worth reporting. So the clock can already have been running for two days by the time anyone opens the policy, because the first two days went on working out what had actually happened. That is the ordinary sequence rather than anyone's failure, which is exactly why it catches people out.

Where notice is written as a condition, late notice can hand the carrier an argument it would not otherwise have had, on a loss that sits squarely inside the cover. I am not telling you what your policy says. Only that the question is time-sensitive, and that nobody in the first hour of an incident is thinking about it.

1.2

Consent to expenses and the approved-vendor condition

Notice is one condition. Consent is a separate one. Many policies make the payment of incident-response costs conditional on the carrier's prior written consent, and many run a panel: approved breach counsel, approved forensic vendors, sometimes approved notification providers as well. The wordings differ, and the differences are the whole question. Some allow a non-panel vendor with consent that is not to be unreasonably withheld. Others will pay one, but only at the negotiated panel rate. Some exclude anything incurred before consent, full stop.

Hiring outside that structure can reduce or void cover you have already paid for. The exposure is not the size of my invoice. It is the cover that invoice was supposed to sit inside.

1.3

Containment does not wait for the carrier. Procurement does.

Nothing on this page should slow down the first hour. Isolating a host, suspending backup expiry and replication, raising log sizes, exporting the audit window out of a tenant, pulling a returned laptop out of the reimage queue: none of that needs the carrier's approval, and none of it should wait for a call to be returned. Isolation order is still a decision rather than a reflex on a domain controller, a storage head, an industrial controller or a clinical device. Those steps, and that caveat, are set out at /emergency, written so your own staff can do them without me.

What should wait is signature. The engagement letter, the purchase order, the authority to spend: that is procurement, and procurement is the part the policy governs. The two jobs come apart easily enough once you see them as two. The common failure is treating them as one, and then doing neither.

1.4

The hotline usually reaches counsel faster than your own escalation chain

Many carriers and brokers run a staffed incident line that routes an insured to breach counsel quickly, and commonly outside business hours. In practice it often beats an organisation's own escalation path at producing that organisation's own general counsel on a Sunday evening.

Getting counsel in first is what makes it possible to structure the forensic work to support a privilege claim. It does not make the work privileged. Forensic reports are not automatically work product, courts have ordered them produced, and I can structure what I do to support a claim without promising that the claim holds. It is an uncomfortable thing to say, and I am not going to soften it.

The number is ordinarily printed on the policy schedule. If you cannot find it, your broker has it, and while you have the broker on the phone it is worth asking what hours the line is actually staffed. Making a notification does not commit you to pursuing a claim. Whether a notification of circumstance carries any consequence at renewal is a question for the broker, and a fair one to put in the same call.

2

What to put in front of the broker

These are the provisions that decide who pays for a forensic examination and on what terms. I describe them generally, because wordings vary so widely and because I will not characterise any particular carrier's requirements. Read your own policy, or get the broker to read it with you.

Provisions that govern instructing an examiner
ProvisionWhat to establishWhat this does not establish
Notice The window, what event starts it, whom notice must be given to, and in what form. That giving notice preserves cover. A late-notice argument turns on the wording and the facts, and notice given correctly settles only the notice question.
Consent to expenses Whether costs require prior written consent, and whether anything incurred before consent is recoverable at all. That consent to one vendor's costs extends to another's, to a later phase of the same matter, or to a ceiling increase.
Panel counsel and approved vendors Whether the panel is mandatory or default, and what the route is for using someone off it. That a firm's presence on a panel is a statement about the quality of its work, or that its absence is a statement about mine.
Retention or deductible The amount, and whether forensic cost sits inside it. What the eventual total will be. The retention is a threshold, not an estimate of the work.
Sublimits Whether incident response, forensics or notification carries its own sublimit below the policy limit. That work stops when a sublimit is exhausted. A sublimit caps what is reimbursed, not what is examined; whether the work continues past it is your authorisation decision, taken against the written ceiling, and not the carrier's.
Rate treatment off panel Whether a non-panel vendor is paid at its own rate, at panel rates, or not at all. What my rate would be. It is published at /rates and it does not move according to who is paying it.
Definitions How the policy defines incident, claim, and the trigger — discovery, first suspicion, or determination. Which definition your facts fall under. That is a coverage question for counsel and the broker, and I do not answer it.

One closing question for the broker: if you instruct a non-panel examiner today, what specifically happens to the claim. Ask it in those words. The answer is knowable in advance, and it is a great deal harder to get once the invoices exist.

3

My panel position, stated plainly

I am not on any carrier's approved-vendor panel. I hold no pre-approval, no framework agreement and no status with any carrier or broker that would answer your consent question in advance.

I say it here, on my own site, in the plainest wording I can find, because the alternative is an insured discovering it after the work has been done. Leaving the question vague moves a coverage risk onto the client in order to win the engagement. That is not a trade I will make.

3.1

The three positions where an off-panel instruction is realistic

Where this practice realistically fits
PositionHow it worksWhat this does not establish
Subcontract or second-examiner work for a panel firm The panel firm holds the engagement and the carrier relationship. I take a defined workstream, or act as the second examiner where the volume, the geography or an internal conflict makes that sensible. My work product carries my name and my method, and I put in writing what I did and what I did not do. That I am approved by, associated with, or endorsed by any carrier or panel firm, or that any such arrangement is in place. It is a position I will accept, not a relationship I hold out. Where one exists on your matter it is named in the engagement letter, and it confers nothing beyond itself.
The insured's own examiner Where an insured wants an examiner instructed by, and reporting to, the insured and its counsel rather than through the panel, I will take that instruction, with the consent question resolved first, in writing, by the insured and the broker. That the cost is recoverable. Whether a carrier reimburses an examiner it did not appoint is a coverage question I take no position on, and I will not encourage an insured to fund work outside the policy in order to keep it with me.
Matters below a panel firm's floor A single laptop. One mailbox. A departing employee, or a wire fraud sitting under the retention. Real work, and too small for a panel consultancy to mobilise on. Sometimes there is no claim at all and the question is only what happened. That the matter is small in consequence. Something under a retention can still produce a notification obligation, a termination, or litigation, and I scope it on that basis rather than on the invoice.
3.2

What I will and will not do with your carrier

If the carrier or breach counsel wants to assess me, I answer their questions directly and give them whatever they reasonably need: the method document at /method, the published rate schedule, my conflicts position. If they decline, that is their decision. I will not lobby them, go round them to the insured, or press you to instruct me anyway. And if a panel firm is instructed instead and wants a defined piece of the work done by me, I am available for that.

None of this has to be worked out during an incident. The consent and conflicts questions can be settled while nothing is on fire, which is what the standby agreement at /standby is for. A pre-cleared vendor position is one of the things worth doing with your broker in the room.

4

What I will not do where an insurer is involved

I will not

  1. Take or pay a referral fee, in either direction, to a broker, a carrier, breach counsel, or anyone else who sends me work. There is no arrangement of any kind between this practice and any insurer or intermediary.
  2. Tell you that your loss is covered, that your notice is timely, that a cost will be reimbursed, or that an incident meets a policy definition. Those are coverage questions and they belong with your counsel and your broker.
  3. Advise you to delay a notification, or to describe an incident in a particular way so that it falls inside or outside a definition. What I write about an incident is what the artefacts support, and I write it knowing a carrier may read it.
  4. Represent myself to a carrier, a broker or an insured as panel-approved, pre-approved, pre-qualified, or as holding any status I do not hold.
  5. Vary my rate according to whether an insurer, a law firm or an individual is paying it. The schedule at /rates is the schedule, and a matter with a carrier behind it is not priced differently from one without.
  6. Bill a carrier directly on the footing that the retaining party is off the hook if the carrier declines. My client is the party that signs the engagement letter. Who reimburses that party is between them and their insurer, and I say so before the work starts rather than in a collections letter afterwards.
5

My own insurance position, and how to ask about it

Vendor onboarding at a law firm or a corporate insured commonly requires evidence of professional liability and cyber cover at stated limits, sometimes with additional-insured or waiver-of-subrogation wording. What satisfies that requirement is a certificate issued by the carrier, not an assertion from the vendor.

I do not publish my position on this page. A sentence on a website is not evidence of cover. A certificate from the carrier is, and it is the only form of the answer that is worth anything to your procurement team. Put the question to me directly before you engage me and I will answer it in writing: whether cover is in place, at what limits, what form of evidence is available and from whom, and any onboarding requirement I cannot meet. Where my position does not satisfy your requirement, I will tell you that instead of sending you a document that does not.

The same discipline applies to every other condition. Where an engagement requires a certified examiner, an accredited laboratory, or a licence in your venue, that condition is identified at scoping, named in the engagement letter, and either satisfied before work begins or the matter is declined. I check the licensing position for your venue and either hold it, work under one, or decline the matter. There is no state-by-state table on this site; the map is contested and it moves.

6

If the carrier directs you elsewhere

Then go. An insured who follows the policy and instructs the panel vendor has made the right decision, and it stays the right decision even where I would have been glad of the work. If the panel firm turns out to be the wrong size for the matter, or you later want a second examiner over the same artefacts, that conversation can be had at that point, with the consent question settled first.

If I am already on the phone when you discover the panel condition, I stop. The scoping call is free. I do not charge for the call that ends with me telling you to ring your carrier, and nothing said on it creates an engagement. Where a preliminary approach has been logged for conflicts purposes, the logging creates no engagement either, and what was said on the call stays confidential whether or not you ever instruct me.

7

This is not coverage advice

Nothing on this page is legal advice, coverage advice, or advice about your particular policy, and none of it creates a relationship between us. I am not a broker and not an adjuster, and I do not act for insurers. Wordings differ from carrier to carrier, and from year to year within the same programme, so a general description of a common condition is not a description of yours. The only authority on what your policy requires is your policy, read with your broker and your counsel.

The whole of the recommendation is one sentence: make the call to the carrier or the broker before you instruct anyone, and make it in parallel with containment rather than after it.
