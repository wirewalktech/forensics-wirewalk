EDRM is shared vocabulary, not a standard

The Electronic Discovery Reference Model is a diagram of stages that most people in this field use to talk to each other. It is not a standard, it is not required by the Federal Rules, and it is not linear — in practice a matter moves backwards through it as often as forwards, because what processing reveals changes what should have been collected. We use its vocabulary on this page so that a litigation support manager reading it can map our lines onto their own workflow. We do not treat conformity with a diagram as evidence of anything.

This page describes what we do at each stage, what the deliverable looks like, and what it costs per unit. It does not tell you what your obligations are. When a discovery obligation attached, what has to be preserved, what has to be produced and what can properly be withheld are questions for your lawyer, and we work to the instruction rather than supplying it.

2

Identification

Identification is an interview and a set of console queries, and it produces a document rather than an opinion. We ask custodians and administrators what exists, then check the answer against what the tenant, the directory and the endpoint management console actually report, because those two lists can differ, and the gap between them is where unnamed sources sit. Personal accounts used for work, departed-employee mailboxes still on a deletion timer, chat platforms nobody thinks of as a record system, shared drives inherited from a merged entity and the backup estate itself are the categories that go unnamed most often.

The output records, per source, what it is, who controls it, how long it is retained, what licence tier governs the audit record, and the date by which it must be preserved or lost. That last column is the useful one. Sources do not expire on the same day, and a list ordered by expiry is a list that can be worked.

3

Preservation and legal hold

We apply holds on instruction. In a cloud tenant that ordinarily means in-platform holds, suspension of retention and expiry policies, suspension of backup expiry, garbage collection and replication, and an audit log export taken out of the tenant while the window is still open. On endpoints it means removing devices from the return-and-reimage queue before anything else, and documenting the state each was found in.

We produce a hold confirmation report: what was placed on hold, where, by which mechanism, at what time, and which sources could not be held and why. Where counsel wants us to track custodian notices and acknowledgements, we will run the tracking; the notices themselves come from counsel.

3.1

The limits of a hold, stated before it is applied

A hold placed today preserves what still exists today. It does not recover what a retention cycle deleted last month, and enabling an audit setting now logs forward only. Where a source was never configured to record the thing you need, there is no version of preservation that fixes it retrospectively, and we will tell you that at the point we place the hold rather than at the point you ask for the data.

If ESI has already been lost, what we report is what the artefacts show: what existed, when it was deleted, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored or replaced from backups, shadow copies, journals or a second custodian. FRCP 37(e) is the exclusive framework for that question, and its subsection (e)(2) turns on a finding that a party acted with the intent to deprive another of the information. That finding belongs to the court. We do not offer it, and we say so plainly when routine retention deletion and deliberate destruction cannot be distinguished in the artefacts — which is often.

4

Collection

Collection is priced per device, per custodian, per endpoint and per hour — never per gigabyte. A per-GB collection price invites an argument about whether the gigabytes were counted before or after processing, and that argument is worth more to the vendor than to the client. The published prices are on the rate schedule.

Most eDiscovery collections should be targeted rather than full-disk. A targeted set — mailbox, drive, chat export, a defined artefact set from an endpoint using tooling of the KAPE or Velociraptor class — answers most discovery questions at a fraction of the downstream processing and hosting cost, because everything collected becomes billable volume later. Full forensic images are the right answer when the question is about the device itself: deletion, connection of external media, anti-forensic activity, timeline reconstruction. We will tell you which question you are asking before we choose.

Every collection set is hashed at the earliest possible point, carries a manifest, records the authority relied on for each account and device, and carries a written note of what was deliberately excluded. Authority is confirmed before collection, not after; where it is unclear, we decline until it is resolved. The handling and custody terms are on evidence handling.

5

Processing

Processing takes the collected set and makes it reviewable: containers and archives expanded, attachments extracted as separate documents with their family relationships recorded, text and metadata extracted, OCR applied where there is no extractable text, deduplication applied at the level counsel specifies, and known system files removed against the NIST reference set.

Two outputs matter more than the rest. The first is the exception report, which lists by count and by path everything that did not process: encrypted containers, password-protected files, corrupt items, unsupported formats, files that OCR could not read. Exceptions get resolved, re-run or reported; they do not get quietly dropped, and the count of what went in has to reconcile against the count of what came out. The second is the processing log itself, which records the tool, the version, the settings applied, the deduplication scope and the timestamps — the record that lets someone else establish that the reviewable set corresponds to the collected set.

6

Analysis and culling

Before anything is hosted, we model the cull. Date range, custodian scope, domain exclusion, file-type exclusion, deduplication scope, near-duplicate and email-thread suppression each remove volume, and each one is a decision with a defensibility cost as well as a money saving. We put the options in front of counsel with the volume consequence and the monthly hosting consequence of each stated in advance, and counsel decides which ones are safe to apply in the matter.

Where technology-assisted review is in play, the protocol is designed and validated as a separate, fixed-price piece of work, with the sample sizes, the measured recall and precision, and the stop criteria written down before the review starts rather than reconstructed after it. What a validated protocol establishes is how the classifier performed against a measured sample. It does not establish that every responsive document was found. No review methodology establishes that, including a linear human one.

Production

A production is a delivered set of files plus the metadata that lets the receiving party load it. Most disputes about productions are disputes about that second part.

7.2

Privilege, filtering and the log

We operate a documented filter protocol: potentially privileged material is segregated before substantive review, flagged rather than adjudicated, and escalated to counsel or to a court-appointed special master. The log output is built to meet FRCP 26(b)(5)(A) — a description of the withheld material sufficient to let the other parties assess the claim without disclosing the protected content itself.

Log format is now a question for the discovery plan rather than an end-of-review argument. As amended effective 2025-12-01, FRCP 26(f)(3)(D) requires the plan to state the parties' views and proposals on the timing and method for complying with 26(b)(5)(A), and FRCP 16(b)(3)(B)(iv) permits — it does not require — the scheduling order to set that timing and method. We will attend that technical conference and say what a given format costs to produce, because a log format agreed without reference to what the processing platform can export is a format someone rebuilds by hand later. Where the court will enter an order under FRE 502(d), we work under it and say so.

We do not describe ourselves as a taint team. A vendor asserting the privilege call is a worse arrangement than a government filter team, and those have drawn sharp judicial criticism.

8

Form of production, and when we will argue with the request

Under FRCP 34(b)(1)(C) a request may specify the form or forms in which ESI is to be produced, and under FRCP 34(b)(2)(D) a party objecting to a specified form must state the form it intends to use. Where no form is specified, FRCP 34(b)(2)(E)(ii) requires production in a form in which the information is ordinarily maintained, or in a reasonably usable form. FRCP 34(b)(2)(E)(iii) provides that a party need not produce the same ESI in more than one form.

Our part in that is technical and narrow. We produce in the form your lawyer specifies. What we add is a written statement, before production rather than after, of what a requested form will cost the receiving party in usability — a spreadsheet imaged to page format loses its formulae, a chat thread imaged loses its sequence, a database imaged loses everything that made it a database. Whether that degradation matters in your matter is counsel's judgement and the court's. Our part is to put it in writing before the set goes out, rather than leave it to be discovered from the production itself.

9

Proportionality is a cost argument, and cost arguments need numbers

Under FRCP 26(b)(1) the scope of discovery is matter that is relevant to a party's claim or defence and proportional to the needs of the case, considering the importance of the issues at stake, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access to relevant information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit. Separately, FRCP 26(b)(2)(B) allows a party to identify sources of ESI that are not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or cost, with the burden of that showing on the responding party.

Both of those arguments are made with figures, and the figures are ours: the volume at each source, what restoration or collection would take, what processing and hosting the resulting volume would cost per month, and what the alternative scopes would cost instead. We supply the measured numbers and the method by which they were measured. Whether the burden outweighs the benefit is an argument counsel makes and a court decides, and we do not characterise a request as disproportionate on your behalf.

10

The economics, and where we measure

Processing is billed per gigabyte of source volume as collected — pre-expansion, pre-deduplication. That is the number you can count on your own drive before you hand it over, and the number on the collection manifest we give you at the point of collection.

The industry convention is different. Most processing is metered on expanded volume: the figure after containers are unpacked, archives are opened, attachments are broken out into separate documents, mail stores are exploded into individual messages and forensic images are extracted to their contents. Expansion is real work and there is a coherent argument for billing it. But expansion multiplies, the multiplier is not knowable before the data is processed, and the client cannot check it against anything they hold. A quote given on source volume and invoiced on expanded volume is not a quote.

The measurement point is chosen against our own interest. Source volume as collected is the smaller number in almost every matter, and the difference is revenue we do not bill. We publish it because the alternative is a unit price that cannot be checked, and an invoice a client cannot check is an invoice that becomes an argument. If the rate does not cover the cost of the work, the rate moves. The measurement point does not.

Two consequences follow, and both are on our side of the ledger. Everything collected becomes billable volume, so over-collection earns us money — which is why collection scope is discussed before the collection rather than after. And deduplication, which usually removes a large fraction of a mail-heavy set, happens after the point we measure, so we bill the duplicates. We would rather say that here than have it noticed on the invoice.

10.2

The culling conflict, stated plainly

Our per-GB revenue is a function of how much data reaches processing and how long it stays hosted. Targeted collection reduces the first. Culling reduces both. On a mail-heavy matter the difference between a broad collection hosted for a year and a scoped one culled before hosting can be the difference between a five-figure bill and a four-figure one.

So the incentive is exactly backwards. The bill we would otherwise send you is the larger one, and every recommendation in clauses 4 and 6 that saves you money costs us money. There is no structural fix for that; a vendor who bills on volume and advises on volume is conflicted whatever they write on a website. What we can do is name it, tell you when it applies, and put the volume and cost consequence of each culling option in writing before it is applied, so the decision is yours and is made on numbers rather than on our recommendation alone.

If you want the conflict removed rather than disclosed, ask us to quote the project management hourly and have a third party host. We will tell you when that is cheaper for you, and it sometimes is.

11

Ask which economics you are buying

The most expensive mistake in this area is not choosing the wrong vendor. It is running a matter through the wrong cost model. Forensic examination is hourly against a written ceiling because the deliverable is an answer. eDiscovery is per gigabyte and per month because the deliverable is a processed, hosted, produced volume. A breach notification exercise can be either, depending on how it is framed, and the difference between the two framings is the whole bill.

Ask us on the first call which one you are buying. The scoping call is free, including the version of it that ends with us saying the data cannot answer your question, or that you need a review provider rather than an examiner.

On eDiscovery matters, we will not

  1. Bill you for expanded volume. Processing is measured on the data as we collected it, pre-expansion and pre-deduplication.
  2. Image a whole disk where a targeted collection answers the question.
  3. Tell you when culling would take your bill from five figures to four and then not do it — even though the bill we would otherwise send you is the larger one.
  4. Take a commission from any platform or hosting vendor we place. Review platform licences are passed through at cost, with no mark-up in either direction.
  5. Bill an hour above the ceiling you authorised in writing.
  6. Adjudicate privilege. We flag and segregate; counsel or the court decides.
  7. Opine that a party acted with intent to deprive another of ESI. We report what the artefacts show. That finding is the court's.
  8. Produce in a form we know will degrade usability without telling you first.
  9. Take a matter we cannot finish inside your production deadline. We will say so on the first call rather than accept it and miss it.
