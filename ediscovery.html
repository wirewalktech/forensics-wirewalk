EDRM is shared vocabulary, not a standard

The Electronic Discovery Reference Model is a diagram of stages that most people in this field use to talk to each other. It is not a standard, and the Federal Rules do not require it. It is not linear either. A matter moves backwards through it about as often as forwards, because what processing turns up keeps changing what should have been collected. I use its vocabulary here so that a litigation support manager can map my lines onto the workflow they already run, and for no other reason. I do not treat conformity with a diagram as evidence of anything.

What follows is what I do at each stage, what the deliverable looks like, and what it costs per unit. It does not tell you what your obligations are. When a discovery obligation attached, what has to be preserved, what has to be produced and what can properly be withheld are questions for your lawyer. I work to the instruction rather than supplying it.

2

Identification

Identification is an interview and a set of console queries, and what it produces is a document rather than an opinion. I ask custodians and administrators what exists. Then I check the answer against what the tenant, the directory and the endpoint management console actually report, because those two lists can come apart, and the gap between them is where the unnamed sources sit. The ones that go unnamed most often are personal accounts used for work, a departed employee's mailbox still sitting on a deletion timer, a chat platform nobody thinks of as a record system, and a shared drive inherited from a merged entity. And the backup estate, which almost nobody volunteers.

The output records, per source, what it is, who controls it, how long it is retained, what licence tier governs the audit record, and the date by which it has to be preserved or it is gone. That last column is the one people actually use. Sources do not all expire on the same day, and a list ordered by expiry is a list somebody can work down in order.

3

Preservation and legal hold

I apply holds on instruction. In a cloud tenant that ordinarily means in-platform holds, suspension of retention and expiry policies, suspension of backup expiry, garbage collection and replication, and an audit log export taken out of the tenant while the window is still open. On endpoints the first job is getting devices out of the return-and-reimage queue, before anyone has a chance to be helpful with them. Then documenting the state each one was found in.

What you get back is a hold confirmation report: what was placed on hold, where, by which mechanism, at what time, and which sources could not be held and why. Where counsel wants custodian notices and acknowledgements tracked, I will run the tracking. The notices themselves come from counsel.

3.1

The limits of a hold, stated before it is applied

A hold placed today preserves what still exists today. It does not recover what a retention cycle deleted last month, and switching on an audit setting now logs forward only. If a source was never configured to record the thing you need, no amount of preservation fixes that retrospectively. I say so while I am placing the hold, rather than at the point you ask me for the data.

If ESI has already been lost, what I report is what the artefacts show: what existed, when it was deleted, by what mechanism, and whether it can be restored or replaced from backups, shadow copies, journals or a second custodian. FRCP 37(e) is the exclusive framework for that question, and its subsection (e)(2) turns on a finding that a party acted with the intent to deprive another of the information. That finding belongs to the court. I do not offer it. And where routine retention deletion cannot be distinguished from deliberate destruction in the artefacts, I say so, which is more often than anyone would like.

4

Collection

I price collection per device, per custodian, per endpoint and per hour. Never per gigabyte. A per-GB collection price invites an argument about whether the gigabytes were counted before processing or after, and that argument is worth a great deal more to the vendor than to the client. The published prices are on the rate schedule.

Most eDiscovery collections should be targeted rather than full-disk. A mailbox, a drive, a chat export, a defined artefact set pulled off an endpoint with tooling of the KAPE or Velociraptor class: that answers most discovery questions at a fraction of the downstream processing and hosting cost, because everything collected becomes billable volume later. A full forensic image earns its cost when the question is about the device itself rather than about the documents on it, which usually means deletion, external media, anti-forensic activity, or a timeline that has to be reconstructed. Before I choose, I will tell you which of those two questions I think you are asking.

Every collection set is hashed at the earliest point the technology allows. It carries a manifest, a record of the authority relied on for each account and device, and a written note of what was deliberately excluded. Authority is confirmed before collection, not after. Where it is unclear I stop until it is resolved, which is an awkward thing to say with the kit already unpacked in somebody's server room, and it is still the right answer. The handling and custody terms are on evidence handling.

5

Processing

Processing takes the collected set and makes it reviewable. Containers and archives are expanded, attachments are extracted as separate documents with their family relationships recorded, text and metadata come out, OCR is applied where there is no extractable text, deduplication runs at the level counsel specifies, and known system files are removed against the NIST reference set.

Two of the outputs matter more than the rest. The exception report lists, by count and by path, everything that did not process: encrypted containers, password-protected files, corrupt items, unsupported formats, anything OCR could not read. Those get resolved, re-run or reported. They do not get quietly dropped, and the count of what went in has to reconcile against the count of what came out. The processing log is the other one. It records the tool, the version, the settings applied, the deduplication scope and the timestamps, so that somebody else can establish that the reviewable set corresponds to the collected set. It is dull to produce, and it is the record everything else rests on.

6

Analysis and culling

Before anything is hosted, I model the cull. Date range, custodian scope, domain exclusion, file-type exclusion, deduplication scope, near-duplicate and email-thread suppression each take volume out, and each one is a decision that carries a defensibility cost as well as a saving. I put the options in front of counsel with the volume consequence and the monthly hosting consequence of each written down in advance. Counsel decides which ones are safe to apply in the matter. Some of them will not be.

Where technology-assisted review is in play, I design and validate the protocol as a separate, fixed-price piece of work, with the sample sizes, the measured recall and precision and the stop criteria written down before the review starts rather than reconstructed afterwards. A validated protocol establishes how the classifier performed against a measured sample. It does not establish that every responsive document was found. No review methodology establishes that, including a linear human one.

Production

A production is a delivered set of files plus the metadata that lets the receiving party load it. Most disputes about productions are disputes about that second part.

7.2

Privilege, filtering and the log

I operate a documented filter protocol. Potentially privileged material is segregated before substantive review, flagged rather than adjudicated, and escalated to counsel or to a court-appointed special master. The log output is built to meet FRCP 26(b)(5)(A): a description of the withheld material sufficient to let the other parties assess the claim, without disclosing the protected content itself.

Log format is now a question for the discovery plan rather than an end-of-review argument. As amended effective 2025-12-01, FRCP 26(f)(3)(D) requires the plan to state the parties' views and proposals on the timing and method for complying with 26(b)(5)(A), and FRCP 16(b)(3)(B)(iv) permits, but does not require, the scheduling order to set that timing and method. I will attend that technical conference and say what a given format costs to produce. A log format agreed without reference to what the processing platform can actually export is a format somebody rebuilds by hand later, usually a junior associate, usually the week it is due. Where the court will enter an order under FRE 502(d), I work under it and say so.

I do not describe myself as a taint team. A vendor asserting the privilege call is a worse arrangement than a government filter team, and those have drawn sharp judicial criticism.

8

Form of production, and when I will argue with the request

Under FRCP 34(b)(1)(C) a request may specify the form or forms in which ESI is to be produced, and under FRCP 34(b)(2)(D) a party objecting to a specified form must state the form it intends to use. Where no form is specified, FRCP 34(b)(2)(E)(ii) requires production in a form in which the information is ordinarily maintained, or in a reasonably usable form. FRCP 34(b)(2)(E)(iii) provides that a party need not produce the same ESI in more than one form.

My part in that is technical and narrow. I produce in the form your lawyer specifies. What I add is a written statement, before production rather than after, of what a requested form will cost the receiving party in usability. A spreadsheet imaged to page format loses its formulae. A database imaged to page format loses everything that made it a database. Whether that degradation matters in your matter is counsel's judgement and the court's, and my job is to put it in writing before the set goes out instead of leaving it to be discovered from the production itself.

9

Proportionality is a cost argument, and cost arguments need numbers

Under FRCP 26(b)(1) the scope of discovery is matter that is relevant to a party's claim or defence and proportional to the needs of the case, considering the importance of the issues at stake, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access to relevant information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit. Separately, FRCP 26(b)(2)(B) allows a party to identify sources of ESI that are not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or cost, with the burden of that showing on the responding party.

Both of those arguments are made with figures, and the figures are mine: the volume at each source, what restoration or collection would take, what processing and hosting the resulting volume would cost per month, and what the alternative scopes would cost instead. I supply the measured numbers and the method by which they were measured. Whether the burden outweighs the benefit is an argument counsel makes and a court decides. I do not characterise a request as disproportionate on your behalf.

10

The economics, and where I measure

Processing is billed per gigabyte of source volume as collected, pre-expansion and pre-deduplication. That is a number you can count on your own drive before you hand it over, and it is the number printed on the collection manifest you get at the point of collection.

The industry convention is different. Most processing is metered on expanded volume, meaning the figure after containers are unpacked, archives opened, attachments broken out into separate documents, mail stores exploded into individual messages and forensic images extracted to their contents. Expansion is real work and there is a coherent argument for billing on it. I am not pretending otherwise. But expansion multiplies, the multiplier is not knowable before the data is processed, and the client cannot check it against anything they hold. A quote given on source volume and invoiced on expanded volume is not a quote.

The measurement point is chosen against my own interest. Source volume as collected is the smaller number in almost every matter, and the difference is revenue I do not bill. I publish it because the alternative is a unit price nobody can check, and an invoice a client cannot check turns into an argument sooner or later. If the rate does not cover the cost of the work, the rate moves. The measurement point does not.

Two consequences follow, and both sit on my side of the ledger. Everything collected becomes billable volume, so over-collection earns me money, which is the reason collection scope gets discussed before the collection rather than after it. Deduplication is the other one. It usually removes a large fraction of a mail-heavy set, and it happens after the point I measure, so I bill you for the duplicates. I would rather write that here than have you find it on the invoice.

10.2

The culling conflict, stated plainly

My per-GB revenue is a function of how much data reaches processing and how long it stays hosted. Targeted collection reduces the first. Culling reduces both. On a mail-heavy matter, the gap between a broad collection hosted for a year and a scoped one culled before hosting is the gap between a five-figure bill and a four-figure one.

So my incentive runs backwards, and this is the part of the page I found hardest to write. Every recommendation in clauses 4 and 6 that saves you money costs me money, and there is no structural fix for that. A vendor who bills on volume and advises on volume is conflicted whatever they put on a website, mine included. What I can do is name it, tell you when it is biting, and put the volume and the cost consequence of each culling option in writing before any of it is applied, so that the decision is yours and is made on numbers rather than on my say-so.

If you would rather have the conflict removed than disclosed, ask me to quote the project management hourly and put the hosting with a third party. I will tell you when that works out cheaper for you. Sometimes it does.

11

Ask which economics you are buying

The most expensive mistake in this area is not choosing the wrong vendor. It is running a matter through the wrong cost model. Forensic examination is hourly against a written ceiling, because the deliverable is an answer. eDiscovery is per gigabyte and per month, because the deliverable is a processed, hosted, produced volume. A breach notification exercise can be framed as either, and the difference between the two framings is the whole bill.

So ask me on the first call which one you are buying. The scoping call is free, including the version of it that ends with me telling you the data cannot answer your question, or that what you need is a review provider and not an examiner.

On eDiscovery matters, I will not

  1. Bill you for expanded volume. Processing is measured on the data as I collected it, pre-expansion and pre-deduplication.
  2. Image a whole disk where a targeted collection answers the question.
  3. Tell you when culling would take your bill from five figures to four and then not do it, even though the bill I would otherwise send you is the larger one.
  4. Take a commission from any platform or hosting vendor I place. Review platform licences are passed through at cost, with no mark-up in either direction.
  5. Bill an hour above the ceiling you authorised in writing.
  6. Adjudicate privilege. I flag and segregate; counsel or the court decides.
  7. Opine that a party acted with intent to deprive another of ESI. I report what the artefacts show. That finding is the court's.
  8. Produce in a form I know will degrade usability without telling you first.
  9. Take a matter I cannot finish inside your production deadline. I will say so on the first call rather than accept it and miss it.
