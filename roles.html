The role is a decision, and it is made first

The same examination can be commissioned in four different ways. The technical work barely changes between them. What changes is who instructs us, who we report to, what an opponent can later demand, and what we are obliged to hand over without being asked.

We ask which role applies on the first call, before any artefact is touched. The material an examiner generates in the first week — notes, search terms, discarded hypotheses, interim positions — is generated under whatever role was in force at the time, and a matter that begins as an internal enquiry and becomes a testifying engagement carries all of it forward.

We do not decide the role. Your lawyer does, and the decision is a legal one about privilege, disclosure and litigation posture. What we do is make the consequences of each choice explicit before the choice is made, and then work consistently with it.

1.1

Who the client is, in each case

The client is the party that signs the engagement letter and is named in it. Where counsel retains us on behalf of a party, counsel is the client and the party is the beneficiary; where a company retains us directly, the company is the client and its lawyers are not. This matters for who can instruct us to stop, who can direct destruction of material, and who receives the report. It is written into the engagement letter in every role, including the neutral one, where the appointing order governs instead.

3

Testifying expert

A testifying expert is disclosed, produces a report, and is available to be deposed and cross-examined. Under FRCP 26(a)(2)(B) the report must contain a complete statement of the opinions and the bases and reasons for them, the facts or data considered in forming them, any exhibits, the witness's qualifications including a list of all publications authored in the previous ten years, a list of all other cases in which the witness testified as an expert at trial or by deposition during the previous four years, and a statement of the compensation to be paid for the study and testimony. It is prepared and signed by the witness who did the work. 26(a)(2)(C) is the lighter disclosure that applies to an expert witness who is not required to provide a written report. Timing runs under 26(a)(2)(D) absent a stipulation or a court order, and those defaults are frequently displaced — we work to the date in the scheduling order, not to a general rule.

One word in that rule governs how we work from day one. 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) reaches facts and data considered, not merely those relied upon, so material looked at and set aside is within the disclosure. Our notes, tool inventory with versions, search terms, filter criteria and discarded hypotheses are therefore kept on the assumption that they will be produced, and are written as the work happens rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Where a matter requires a previously qualified testifying expert, that requirement is identified at scoping, named in the engagement letter, and either met by associating a qualified examiner or the matter is declined.

3.1

What is protected, and the honest limit

FRCP 26(b)(4)(B) protects drafts of any report or disclosure, regardless of the form in which the draft is recorded. 26(b)(4)(C) protects communications between the party's attorney and a witness who is required to provide a report under 26(a)(2)(B) — with three carve-outs. It does not reach a testifying witness who is outside the report requirement, which is one reason the disclosure category is settled at engagement rather than assumed. Discovery is permitted into compensation for the study or testimony; into facts or data that the attorney provided and the expert considered in forming opinions; and into assumptions that the attorney provided and the expert relied on.

The limit is in the mechanism. Both protections operate by treating the material as trial-preparation material, which is a qualified protection and not an absolute one. It can be overcome on the showing the rules allow, and it can be waived by what is done with the material afterwards. An expert who forwards a draft to a third party, or whose file mixes counsel's assumptions into the working notes so that the two cannot be separated, has made the protection considerably harder to assert.

So we segregate. Material supplied by counsel is logged on arrival and kept in its own set, distinct from the artefacts and from our own notes, so the three carve-outs can be honoured precisely without sweeping in drafts and communications that are protected.

3.2

What changes about how we work

Everything is written to be read by someone trying to break it. Each sentence in the report maps to something verified, and each finding carries its own limit in the same paragraph rather than in a disclaimer at the end. Where a timeline is produced, we say whether it is an illustrative aid or a computed summary of voluminous material, because the two are treated differently and an examiner who does not say which is producing an argument about the exhibit rather than the evidence. Deposition and testimony time is billed to the retaining party irrespective of the outcome and irrespective of whether the testimony helps them; under FRCP 26(b)(4)(E) the party seeking the discovery ordinarily pays the expert's reasonable fee for time spent responding to it, absent manifest injustice.

4

Consulting expert

A consulting expert is retained in anticipation of litigation or to prepare for trial, and is not expected to testify. Under FRCP 26(b)(4)(D) the facts known and opinions held by such an expert are ordinarily not discoverable — by interrogatory or deposition — except as provided in Rule 35(b) or on a showing of exceptional circumstances under which it is impracticable for the other side to obtain facts or opinions on the same subject by other means.

Two honest limits belong next to that sentence. The shield protects the expert's facts and opinions; it does not make the underlying evidence disappear. A device, a mailbox or a log is discoverable on its own terms no matter who examined it, and a preservation obligation is unaffected by the role we were retained in. And the exceptional-circumstances exception has real content in this field: where an examination consumed the thing being examined — a volatile memory capture, a one-attempt extraction, a live system since rebuilt — the argument that equivalent facts cannot be obtained by other means becomes considerably stronger. We flag that before performing the step, not after.

The role can also change. If counsel later decides to call us, the consulting shield does not follow us across; the work becomes a testifying expert's work, with the obligations in clause 3 applying to it. That is why a consulting engagement is documented to the same standard as a testifying one.

4.1

The deliverable

Typically a written position to counsel rather than a report in the 26(a)(2)(B) form: what the artefacts show, what they do not reach, what further work would cost and what it could and could not resolve. It is written so that it could become the spine of a disclosed report without being rewritten, because the alternative is a document that says one thing in private and something more careful in public.

5

Appointed neutral or court-appointed examiner

A neutral is appointed by both parties jointly, by stipulation, or by the court — under FRE 706 for a court-appointed expert witness, or as a special master under FRCP 53 where the appointment is to supervise a protocol rather than to give an opinion. The appointing instrument, not the engagement letter, governs the scope of work, who receives the findings, and how disputes about the protocol are resolved.

Nothing in this role is protected from the parties or from the court. The protocol, the search terms, the working notes and the findings go to all parties and to the court, subject only to whatever the appointing instrument says about disclosure beyond them. Under FRE 706(b) a court-appointed expert must advise the parties of any findings, may be deposed by any party, may be called to testify by the court or any party, and may be cross-examined by any party — including the party that called the expert. We write the whole file on that basis from the first hour.

We accept an appointed-neutral role only where both parties or the court appoint us, and we do not act as neutral in a matter where we have advised either side.

We ask for three things before accepting. A written, agreed protocol, stating what is searched, what is not, and what happens to material outside the scope. A defined route for privilege: we flag and segregate, we do not adjudicate, and anything flagged goes to counsel or to the court rather than to the requesting party. And the compensation arrangement settled in the order, because a neutral paid by one side is an argument waiting to be made.

6

Rebuttal or second-opinion examiner

This is a testifying or consulting engagement in form — the discovery posture is the one in clause 3 or clause 4 — but the subject matter is another examiner's work rather than the evidence directly. It is worth stating separately because its limits are specific.

A first-pass review marks each material finding as supported, partly supported, unsupported or untestable on the material provided, and sets out the questions to put. The fourth category does most of the work. A finding is untestable where the underlying artefacts were not produced, where the method was not described in enough detail to reproduce, or where the examination itself altered or consumed what would be needed to check it. Untestable is not a synonym for wrong, and we do not let it become one.

Where the review goes further — re-examining the original artefacts rather than the report — it is an examination in its own right, hourly against a written ceiling, and it carries its own limits. One of those is worth naming: where the work under review was an internal IT investigation, part of what has to be assessed is what that investigation itself overwrote, and the answer can be that the question is no longer answerable by anyone.

7

Technical adviser to counsel

Here we do not examine and we do not opine. The work is helping counsel understand what the other side's technical position actually asserts, drafting the technical parts of requests and responses, preparing questions for the deposition of an opposing examiner, reading an ESI protocol for the thing it quietly makes impossible, and advising on what a proposed search would and would not capture. The deliverable is usually a memorandum, a question list or a mark-up.

Two boundaries. The role is intended to stay advisory, and the moment an opinion is disclosed or relied upon the role has changed — we say so rather than letting it drift. And we do not draft the substance of an expert's report for anyone; an examiner signs their own work, in every role on this page.

8

Privilege, and its honest limit

Where privilege matters, counsel usually prefers to retain us directly, scoped to the legal question and paid from the legal budget, with distribution controlled and any business-remediation workstream kept separate from the litigation report. How the engagement is structured is a decision for your lawyer and we will paper it either way. What we will not tell you is that involving us makes anything privileged. Forensic reports are not automatically work product; courts have ordered them produced. We can structure our work to support a privilege claim. We cannot promise one holds.

What we can do is keep the structure clean enough that the claim is capable of being made. Where a single report serves both the litigation question and the business's remediation programme, the argument that it was prepared because of the litigation becomes considerably harder to sustain, and that dual purpose is what courts have examined when ordering a forensic report produced; two workstreams, separately scoped and separately distributed, is not a trick, it is an accurate description of two different pieces of work. We keep a distribution list. We do not circulate beyond it. Where the court will enter an order under FRE 502(d), we work under it and say so, and where privileged material is encountered in the course of an examination we flag and segregate it rather than adjudicating it — the examiner applies the protocol, counsel or the court makes the call.

8.1

State practice differs, and we check rather than assume

The protections described in clause 3.1 and clause 4 are the federal position. State practice is not uniform. Some states have adopted rules that track the 2010 federal amendments on draft reports and attorney–expert communications; some have not, and in those jurisdictions drafts and communications with counsel may be discoverable to a degree they would not be in federal court. Expert disclosure itself can differ in kind rather than in detail — New York state practice runs under CPLR 3101(d), which does not itself require a written report of the federal kind, and calls instead for a statement in reasonable detail of the subject matter, the substance of the facts and opinions, the expert's qualifications and a summary of the grounds for the opinion. Individual courts add to that — the Commercial Division rules on expert disclosure are the ones most likely to reach a matter of this kind — which is a further reason the requirement is read for the venue rather than assumed from the statute. We check the position for the venue at engagement and state it in the engagement letter. We do not publish a state-by-state table, because the position moves and a stale table is worse than none.

The same discipline applies to the admissibility standard. New York remains a Frye jurisdiction; the federal courts apply FRE 702 as amended effective 2023-12-01. Which standard your venue applies changes what the report has to demonstrate about method, and it is checked at engagement rather than assumed.

9

When the role changes mid-matter

A role can change after the file has been opened, and the failure mode is silence. If counsel decides to disclose us after a period of consulting work, or a party proposes that a jointly instructed examination become a testifying one, we say in writing what that does to the existing file: which material becomes disclosable, which protections do not survive the change, and what cannot be undone. Then counsel decides with the position in front of them.

If a conflict surfaces mid-matter, the role question is settled differently and immediately: work stops, the evidence is sequestered unexamined, the retaining party is told the same day, material is returned or destroyed on instruction, the unearned balance is refunded in full, and nothing learned is disclosed to anyone — including to the party the conflict runs to.

10

In every one of these roles

We will not

  1. Take a fee that depends on what we find, on the outcome of the matter, or on which side retains us. The rate is the same either way and it is set before we look at anything.
  2. Act for both sides in a matter, or act as an appointed neutral where we have advised either side.
  3. Write to a conclusion supplied in advance, or report an inference as a finding because a stronger letter is wanted.
  4. Opine on who was at the keyboard from device evidence alone. A device is not a person. Or opine that a party acted with intent to destroy evidence — we report what the artefacts show, and intent is the court's finding.
  5. Take a matter that requires a certification, a licence or a qualification we do not hold. We identify that at scoping, name it in the engagement letter, and either bring in someone who holds it or decline.
  6. Promise you a finding, a recovered file, an unlocked handset, or that anything we produce will be admitted. Admissibility is the court's decision and no one can guarantee it.
