The role is a decision, and it is made first

The same examination can be commissioned four different ways, and the technical work barely changes between them. What changes is who instructs me, who I report to, what an opponent can later demand, and what I am obliged to hand over before anyone thinks to ask for it.

So I ask which role applies on the first call, before I touch anything. Notes, search terms, interim positions, the hypotheses that went nowhere: all of it is generated under whatever role was in force at the time I wrote it, and a matter that starts as an internal enquiry and turns into a testifying engagement carries the whole file forward with it. That sounds like paperwork until the week it decides what an opponent gets.

I do not decide the role. Your lawyer does, and it is a legal decision about privilege, disclosure and litigation posture rather than a technical one. What I do is set out the consequences of each choice before the choice is made, then work consistently with the one you pick.

1.1

Who the client is, in each case

The client is the party that signs the engagement letter and is named in it. Where counsel retains me on behalf of a party, counsel is the client and the party is the beneficiary. Where a company retains me directly, the company is the client and its lawyers are not. That is not a formality, because it decides who can tell me to stop work, who can direct that material be destroyed, and who receives the report. It is written into the engagement letter in every role, including the neutral one, where the appointing order governs instead.

3

Testifying expert

A testifying expert is disclosed, produces a report, and is available to be deposed and cross-examined. FRCP 26(a)(2)(B) sets out what the report has to contain: a complete statement of the opinions and the bases and reasons for them, the facts or data considered in forming them, any exhibits, the witness's qualifications including a list of all publications authored in the previous ten years, a list of all other cases in which the witness testified as an expert at trial or by deposition during the previous four years, and a statement of the compensation to be paid for the study and testimony. The witness who did the work prepares and signs it. 26(a)(2)(C) is the lighter disclosure that applies to an expert witness who is not required to provide a written report. Timing runs under 26(a)(2)(D) absent a stipulation or a court order, and those defaults are displaced often enough that I work to the date in the scheduling order rather than to the general rule.

One word in that rule governs how I work from the first day. 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) reaches facts and data considered, not merely those relied upon, so the material I looked at and put aside is inside the disclosure too. My notes, the tool inventory with versions, the search terms and filter criteria, the hypotheses I abandoned: I keep all of it on the assumption that it will be produced, and I write it while the work is happening rather than reconstructing it afterwards from memory.

Where a matter requires a previously qualified testifying expert, that requirement is identified at scoping, named in the engagement letter, and either met by associating a qualified examiner or the matter is declined.

3.1

What is protected, and the honest limit

FRCP 26(b)(4)(B) protects drafts of any report or disclosure, regardless of the form in which the draft is recorded. 26(b)(4)(C) protects communications between the party's attorney and a witness who is required to provide a report under 26(a)(2)(B), subject to three carve-outs. It does not reach a testifying witness who falls outside the report requirement, which is one reason the disclosure category is settled at engagement instead of assumed. The carve-outs are these. Discovery is permitted into compensation for the study or testimony; into facts or data that the attorney provided and the expert considered in forming opinions; and into assumptions that the attorney provided and the expert relied on.

The limit is in the mechanism, and it is weaker than most people expect. Both protections operate by treating the material as trial-preparation material, which is a qualified protection and not an absolute one. It can be overcome on the showing the rules allow, and it can be waived by what is done with the material afterwards. An expert who forwards a draft to a third party has made the protection considerably harder to assert. So has an expert whose file mixes counsel's assumptions into the working notes until the two cannot be pulled apart again.

So I segregate. Material supplied by counsel is logged on arrival and kept in its own set, away from the artefacts and away from my own notes, so the three carve-outs can be honoured precisely without sweeping in drafts and communications that are protected. It is more bookkeeping than the work strictly needs, right up to the day someone asks for exactly one of those three categories.

3.2

What changes about how I work

Everything is written to be read by someone trying to break it. Each sentence in the report maps to something verified, and each finding carries its own limit in the same paragraph rather than in a disclaimer at the end. Where I produce a timeline, I say whether it is an illustrative aid or a computed summary of voluminous material, because the two are treated differently and an examiner who leaves it ambiguous has started an argument about the exhibit instead of the evidence. Deposition and testimony time is billed to the retaining party irrespective of the outcome and irrespective of whether the testimony helps them. Under FRCP 26(b)(4)(E) the party seeking the discovery ordinarily pays the expert's reasonable fee for time spent responding to it, absent manifest injustice.

4

Consulting expert

A consulting expert is retained in anticipation of litigation or to prepare for trial, and is not expected to testify. Under FRCP 26(b)(4)(D) the facts known and opinions held by such an expert are ordinarily not discoverable by interrogatory or deposition, except as provided in Rule 35(b) or on a showing of exceptional circumstances under which it is impracticable for the other side to obtain facts or opinions on the same subject by other means.

Two honest limits belong next to that sentence. The first is that the shield covers my facts and opinions, not the evidence. A device, a mailbox or a log is discoverable on its own terms no matter who examined it, and your preservation obligation is unaffected by the role I was retained in. The second is that the exceptional-circumstances exception has real content in this field. Where an examination consumes the thing being examined — a volatile memory capture, a one-attempt extraction, a live system since rebuilt — the argument that equivalent facts cannot be obtained by other means gets considerably stronger. I flag that before I take the step, not after.

And the role can change. If counsel later decides to call me, the consulting shield does not travel across with me; the work becomes a testifying expert's work, with the obligations in clause 3 applying to it. Which is why a consulting engagement is documented to the same standard as a testifying one, even when both of us expect the file will never be read by anyone else.

4.1

The deliverable

Usually a written position to counsel rather than a report in the 26(a)(2)(B) form. What the artefacts show, what they do not reach, what further work would cost and what it could and could not resolve. I write it so that it could become the spine of a disclosed report without being rewritten, because the alternative is a document that says one thing in private and something more careful in public.

5

Appointed neutral or court-appointed examiner

A neutral is appointed by both parties jointly, by stipulation, or by the court: under FRE 706 for a court-appointed expert witness, or as a special master under FRCP 53 where the appointment is to supervise a protocol rather than to give an opinion. The appointing instrument governs, not my engagement letter. It sets the scope of the work, who receives the findings, and how disputes about the protocol are resolved.

Nothing in this role is protected from the parties or from the court. The protocol, the search terms, the working notes, the findings, all of it goes to every party and to the court, subject only to whatever the appointing instrument says about disclosure beyond them. Under FRE 706(b) a court-appointed expert must advise the parties of any findings, may be deposed by any party, may be called to testify by the court or any party, and may be cross-examined by any party, including the party that called the expert. I write the whole file on that basis from the first hour.

I accept an appointed-neutral role only where both parties or the court appoint me, and I do not act as neutral in a matter where I have advised either side.

Before accepting, I ask for a written and agreed protocol: what is searched, what is not, and what happens to material that falls outside the scope. I ask for a defined route for privilege, which means I flag and segregate rather than adjudicate, and anything flagged goes to counsel or to the court instead of to the requesting party. And I ask that the compensation arrangement be settled in the order, because a neutral paid by one side is an argument waiting to be made.

6

Rebuttal or second-opinion examiner

In form this is a testifying or consulting engagement, and the discovery posture is whichever of clause 3 or clause 4 applies. What differs is the subject matter, which is another examiner's work rather than the evidence directly. It gets its own clause here because its limits are specific.

A first-pass review marks each material finding as supported, partly supported, unsupported or untestable on the material provided, and sets out the questions to put. The fourth category does most of the work. A finding is untestable where the underlying artefacts were not produced, where the method was not described in enough detail to reproduce, or where the examination itself altered or consumed what would be needed to check it. Untestable is not a synonym for wrong, and I will not let it drift into one, however much stronger the letter would read.

Where the review goes further and I re-examine the original artefacts rather than the report, that is an examination in its own right, hourly against a written ceiling, and it carries its own limits. One of them is worth naming. Where the work under review was an internal IT investigation, part of what has to be assessed is what that investigation itself overwrote, and the answer is sometimes that the question can no longer be answered by anyone.

7

Technical adviser to counsel

Here I do not examine and I do not opine. The work is helping counsel see what the other side's technical position actually asserts, drafting the technical parts of requests and responses, preparing questions for the deposition of an opposing examiner, and reading an ESI protocol for the thing it quietly makes impossible. Advising on what a proposed search would and would not capture tends to be the same conversation. The deliverable is usually a memorandum, a question list or a mark-up.

Two boundaries on it. The role is meant to stay advisory, and the moment an opinion is disclosed or relied upon it has stopped being advisory, so I say so rather than letting it drift. And I do not draft the substance of an expert's report for anyone. An examiner signs their own work, in every role on this page.

8

Privilege, and its honest limit

Where privilege matters, counsel usually prefers to retain me directly, scoped to the legal question and paid from the legal budget, with distribution controlled and any business-remediation workstream kept separate from the litigation report. How the engagement is structured is a decision for your lawyer and I will paper it either way. What I will not tell you is that involving me makes anything privileged. Forensic reports are not automatically work product; courts have ordered them produced. I can structure my work to support a privilege claim. I cannot promise one holds.

What I can do is keep the structure clean enough that the claim is capable of being made. Where a single report serves both the litigation question and the business's remediation programme, the argument that it was prepared because of the litigation becomes much harder to sustain, and that dual purpose is what courts have examined when ordering a forensic report produced. Two workstreams, separately scoped and separately distributed, is not a trick. It is an accurate description of two different pieces of work. I keep a distribution list and I do not circulate beyond it. Where the court will enter an order under FRE 502(d), I work under it and say so. Where privileged material turns up in the middle of an examination, I flag it and segregate it rather than ruling on it: I apply the protocol, counsel or the court makes the call.

8.1

State practice differs, so I check rather than assume

The protections described in clause 3.1 and clause 4 are the federal position, and state practice is not uniform. Some states have adopted rules that track the 2010 federal amendments on draft reports and attorney–expert communications. Some have not, and in those jurisdictions drafts and communications with counsel may be discoverable to a degree they would not be in federal court.

Expert disclosure can also differ in kind rather than in detail. New York state practice runs under CPLR 3101(d), which does not itself require a written report of the federal kind; it calls instead for a statement in reasonable detail of the subject matter, the substance of the facts and opinions, the expert's qualifications and a summary of the grounds for the opinion. Individual courts then add to that, and the Commercial Division rules on expert disclosure are the ones most likely to reach a matter of this kind. So the requirement is read for the venue rather than assumed from the statute. I check the position for your venue at engagement and state it in the engagement letter. I do not publish a state-by-state table, because the position moves and a stale table is worse than none.

The same discipline applies to the admissibility standard. New York remains a Frye jurisdiction; the federal courts apply FRE 702 as amended effective 2023-12-01. Which standard your venue applies changes what the report has to demonstrate about method, so it is checked at engagement rather than assumed.

9

When the role changes mid-matter

A role can change after the file has been opened, and the failure mode is silence. If counsel decides to disclose me after a period of consulting work, or a party proposes that a jointly instructed examination become a testifying one, I say in writing what that does to the file as it already stands: which material becomes disclosable, which protections do not survive the change, and what cannot be undone. Then counsel decides with the position in front of them rather than in retrospect.

A conflict surfacing mid-matter is settled differently, and immediately. Work stops. The evidence is sequestered unexamined, the retaining party is told the same day, material is returned or destroyed on instruction, and the unearned balance is refunded in full. Nothing I have learned is disclosed to anyone, including to the party the conflict runs to.

10

In every one of these roles

I will not

  1. Take a fee that depends on what I find, on the outcome of the matter, or on which side retains me. The rate is the same either way and it is set before I look at anything.
  2. Act for both sides in a matter, or act as an appointed neutral where I have advised either side.
  3. Write to a conclusion supplied in advance, or report an inference as a finding because a stronger letter is wanted.
  4. Opine on who was at the keyboard from device evidence alone. A device is not a person. Nor will I opine that a party acted with intent to destroy evidence: I report what the artefacts show, and intent is the court's finding.
  5. Take a matter that requires a certification, a licence or a qualification I do not hold. I identify that at scoping, name it in the engagement letter, and either bring in someone who holds it or decline.
  6. Promise you a finding, a recovered file, an unlocked handset, or that anything I produce will be admitted. Admissibility is the court's decision and no one can guarantee it.
