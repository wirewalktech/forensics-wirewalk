1

What this document is

This is the procedure I follow. I publish it in full because the procedure is the part of a forensic engagement that gets attacked, and an examiner who has not written it down before the engagement ends up writing it on the stand. None of it is a claim about past matters. It is a description of how I work, set out in enough detail that an examiner retained against me could take the same media, follow the same steps and say whether my result holds.

Two things it is not. It is not a guarantee that anything here will be admitted; that is the court's decision and nobody can promise it. It is also not a claim of accreditation. The standards named below are guidance I work to, not certificates I hold. Where a matter requires a certified examiner, an accredited laboratory or a licence, I identify that at scoping and state it in the engagement letter, and either the condition is satisfied before work begins or I decline the matter.

1.1

The four principles the method is written against

ISO/IEC 27037:2012 sets out four requirements for the handling of digital evidence, and every step below exists to satisfy one of them. Auditability means an independent assessor can see what was done and why. Repeatability means the same test on the same material under the same conditions gives the same result. Reproducibility means a different examiner, using different equipment, arrives at that result too. Justifiability means every choice can be explained and defended afterwards, including the choices that altered something. The same standard separately sets out three qualities the evidence itself must have, relevance and reliability and sufficiency; I cite the four requirements here because they are the ones you can write a procedure against.

Of the four, repeatability and reproducibility are what decide contested matters. A finding the other side's examiner cannot reproduce is not a finding, it is my word. So the working notes record the filters and search terms I used and the version of every tool, not just what I concluded.

2

Authority and scope, before anything is touched

The first step is not technical. Before a device is connected to anything, I confirm and record who is authorising access to it, what they are authorising access to, and what entitles them to authorise it. Where that is unclear, I decline. I would rather lose the engagement at that point than at the point where the other side asks the question.

It can look like box-ticking. It is not. Access without authority engages the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030. Interception of communications in transit engages the Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2511. And the Stored Communications Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2702, is the reason a civil subpoena to a provider does not produce message content, whatever the subpoena says. State computer-crime and eavesdropping statutes add further constraints and are not uniform. The same few situations cause the trouble every time, so I put them on the table at scoping rather than discover them later: personal devices used for work under a BYOD arrangement; household machines that more than one person uses; accounts where a spouse or a relative or a co-owner supplies the credentials; saved sessions on a company laptop that reach into somebody's personal mail or cloud storage. A credential that works is not authority to use it.

Suspected child sexual abuse material is handled under a stop-and-escalate rule that cannot be varied by agreement. I do not take or retain custody of it, and I work through counsel and law enforcement on access, noting 18 U.S.C. § 3509(m) in federal criminal matters. The full statement is on evidence handling, and clients hear it before they engage me rather than afterwards.

2.1

Scope is written down before collection, not inferred from it

I write down the question the examination is meant to answer, the custodians and systems in scope, the date range, and what is deliberately excluded. Where a targeted collection answers the question, I take the targeted collection, and the manifest records what I chose not to collect and why. Imaging a whole disk is easier to defend than a decision is, which is exactly why I do not do it on that basis, and I do not bill you for processing volume that was never needed.

3

Acquisition

Acquisition is the point at which the evidence either becomes checkable or stops being checkable, and it is almost always unrepeatable. A mistake anywhere after it can be recovered from. A mistake here cannot.

3.1

Write blocking

Where the source permits it, I present the source to the acquisition system through a hardware write blocker, or through a software write block where hardware is not usable for that interface. If I use a software block, the tool record says what validation it has been subjected to and by whom, rather than simply asserting that it works. I confirm the blocked state and record it before imaging starts; a blocker sitting on the bench is not the same thing as a blocker in line, and the presence of the device is not the confirmation. The make, model and firmware or version of the blocker go in the acquisition log next to the imaging tool and its version.

3.2

When write blocking cannot be used, which is often

It is no help for live memory capture, or for a running production system that must not be taken down, or for most mobile extractions. Nor for cloud and SaaS sources, where the collection is an authenticated API session rather than a read of a medium. In all of those the acquisition alters something, and pretending otherwise is worse than the alteration.

Soundness then has to come out of the record, which is why I build it as I go rather than afterwards. The method is documented step by step. The tool and its version are recorded. The output is hashed at the earliest point at which a hash means anything. And the alteration that could not be avoided is stated plainly, with the reason it could not be avoided and an assessment of what it could have affected. A running system changes while you look at it. A report that says so is stronger than one that does not, because the first thing an examiner on the other side will do is go hunting for the artefacts my own tooling wrote.

3.3

Live acquisition, and when it is the right call

Memory is the clearest case. It holds running processes, network connections, injected code and, in an intrusion, often the only copy of what was executed. It is overwritten continuously while the machine runs and it does not survive a power-off at all. If memory bears on the question, the machine has to be taken live or that evidence is simply not there to collect.

Full-volume encryption is the case that decides it more often. A running, unlocked machine with BitLocker or FileVault engaged is readable. The same machine powered off, with no escrowed recovery key, is a block of ciphertext that no amount of effort or expenditure opens. So I establish the encryption state and where the keys are before anything is powered down, and where both memory and disk are in scope I capture memory first, following the order of volatility in NIST SP 800-86.

There is a cost to this and I would rather state it than bury it. Capturing memory from a running system means running code on that system and writing to it. I record what was run, from what medium, at what time, and under what account.

3.4

Mobile and cloud

Mobile and cloud acquisitions are logical and partial. What is obtainable moves with the handset, the operating system version and the security patch level; for cloud, with the tenant's licence tier and retention configuration at the moment of collection. I record the level achieved and the method used, and then the part that matters a year later, which is what was not obtainable at that level. For a cloud collection I also record the authority I relied on and the tenant's retention and licence tier as they stood that day, because both will have changed by the time anyone reads the report.

4

Verification

A digest is computed over the bytes as they are read from the source, in the same pass that writes the image. That is the acquisition digest. Afterwards I compute a second digest over the stored image as a separate operation, preferably with a different tool from the one that wrote it. That is the verification digest. Only the comparison of the two means anything. A tool that reports its own image as verified, using its own arithmetic in its own pass, is testing rather less than it appears to be.

I re-verify the image before analysis begins, and again before anything is produced to another party. SHA-256 is the primary algorithm. The limit belongs in the same paragraph as the claim: a hash match proves the image corresponds to the source as it was read at acquisition. It does not prove the source was unaltered before I arrived, it says nothing about who created any of the data, and it is not a statement about content.

4.2

What a mismatch means, and what I do about it

When a verification digest does not match, work stops. Nothing is analysed from that image. It is never ignored, and it is never quietly re-run until it passes. I record the failed verification with its timestamp, both digests and the tool that produced each, and that record goes into the report whether or not the problem is later resolved.

A mismatch means one of three things, and the whole point of the procedure is to find out which. The source may have read differently on the two occasions, usually because it is failing. The image may have been damaged in transfer or in storage. Or something wrote to the image. I narrow the discrepancy rather than guess at it. Where the image is segmented, per-segment digests localise it. Where the source is still available and still readable, I take a fresh acquisition and compare. Where the medium is failing, I re-acquire with the error handling recorded and the bad sectors enumerated, and I say which regions of the image are not reliable.

A failing drive is the common case and it does not destroy the evidence. What destroys the evidence is a second acquisition that quietly replaces the first with no record that the first ever existed. Where the source is no longer available and the discrepancy cannot be resolved, I report the discrepancy and the limits it places on every finding downstream of it. That is an unattractive paragraph to write. It is a great deal less unattractive than having it drawn out of me later.

4.3

Why MD5 and SHA-1 still appear

They appear because tools still emit them, because legacy case records are full of them, and because a second digest from a second algorithm is a cheap independent check that the image file on disk has not changed since I put it there. I record them where a tool provides them. I never describe them as secure. MD5 and SHA-1 both have practical collision attacks; under NIST SP 800-131A Rev. 2 SHA-1 is disallowed for digital signature generation and permitted only for legacy signature verification, and NIST has since announced the transition away from SHA-1 for all applications. Where a digest is doing evidentiary work, the digest doing it is SHA-256.

5

Working copies

I analyse a working copy. The master image is stored under access control with its own integrity record, and no analysis tool mounts it. Working copies are hash-verified against the master before use, and where anything about a copy's state is uncertain I discard it and make another rather than try to repair it.

The reason is not superstition about touching originals. An analysis suite is a large body of software that writes caches, indexes and recovery files, and the cost of being wrong about which of those writes touched the evidence file is the whole engagement. Working on a copy makes the question moot.

6

Examination

Examination proceeds from the scope question recorded at 2.1. I keep the tool inventory with versions, the search terms and filter criteria applied, the date ranges and time zones used, and the hypotheses tested, including the ones I tested and discarded. Findings are stated alongside the artefacts they rest on, so that another examiner can go to those same artefacts and disagree with my reading of them.

Two disciplines are worth naming, because they are where examinations go wrong. The first is timestamps. I treat a timestamp as evidence about a system's record-keeping rather than as a fact about the world: it depends on time zone, clock drift, NTP state, filesystem semantics and the behaviour of whatever application wrote it. Where two timestamp sources conflict, I say so instead of choosing the convenient one. The second is absence. The absence of an artefact is the absence of an artefact, and it is never evidence that an event did not happen, because retention windows, log rotation, TRIM on solid-state media and ordinary housekeeping all destroy data with no intent behind any of it.

Where I report on destruction, I report what was destroyed, when, and by what mechanism. Routine retention deletion looks a great deal like deliberate destruction in the artefacts, and where the two cannot be distinguished I say that they cannot. Whether a party acted with intent to deprive another of evidence is a finding for the court under FRCP 37(e)(2). I do not offer it.

6.1

Notes are written on the assumption that they will be produced

Contemporaneous notes, the tool inventory, search terms, filter criteria and discarded hypotheses are kept in a form fit to be handed over, because FRCP 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) reaches the facts or data considered by a testifying expert, not merely those relied upon. Material supplied by counsel is segregated and logged separately. That is so the carve-outs in FRCP 26(b)(4)(C), covering compensation, facts or data provided and considered, and assumptions provided and relied on, can be honoured without sweeping in protected communications or draft reports, which are protected under 26(b)(4)(B).

7

Chain of custody

The custody record covers receipt, every named custodian, every transfer with its date and time, the storage conditions between transfers, and the return or destruction at the end. I write it as the work happens. A record reassembled afterwards out of memory and calendar entries does not read like one written at the time, and the people who read these for a living can tell.

7.1

The distinction most people get wrong

Authentication is a comparatively low bar. Under FRE 901(a) the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what it is claimed to be, a question of conditional relevance under FRE 104(b) that turns on whether a reasonable juror could so find, not on whether the judge is persuaded. Illustrations 901(b)(1), (b)(3), (b)(4) and (b)(9) each supply a route, and gaps in a custody record ordinarily go to weight rather than admissibility (United States v. Howard-Arias, 679 F.2d 363 (4th Cir. 1982)).

Which sounds like permission to relax, and is the opposite of it: the low bar is the reason the custody record matters more, not less. Evidence that squeaks past the admissibility threshold with an incoherent custody record arrives in front of the fact-finder already damaged, and every hour of cross-examination spent on where the drive was over a weekend is an hour nobody spends on what was on it. The rule is generous; the jury is not. So I do not overstate the rule in my own favour, and I do not treat "it will come in anyway" as a reason to keep a thinner record.

The foundation for authentication gets built at collection time rather than assembled in the weeks before trial. That is the structural argument in Lorraine v. Markel American Insurance Co., 241 F.R.D. 534 (D. Md. 2007), and it is why the imaging worksheet, the acquisition log and the custody record are produced as the work happens.

7.2

Self-authentication, and what it does not resolve

Two provisions are available here, and they get stated the wrong way round often enough that a lawyer notices immediately when they are. FRE 902(14) covers data copied from an electronic device, storage medium or file and authenticated by a process of digital identification, which is the hash-verified copy. FRE 902(13) covers a record generated by an electronic process or system, which is the log file. Both require a certification by a qualified person meeting the requirements of FRE 902(11) or (12), and 902(11) carries the notice condition: reasonable written notice of the intent to offer the record, with the record and certification made available for inspection in advance.

The limit goes where the capability goes. These provisions remove the need for live foundation testimony on authenticity, and nothing else. They resolve neither hearsay, nor relevance, nor best evidence, and an opponent remains free to contest authenticity.

On best evidence. FRE 1002 read with 1001(d) and 1001(e) means that for electronically stored information an "original" includes any printout or other output readable by sight that accurately reflects the information, and under FRE 1003 a duplicate is admissible to the same extent as the original unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or the circumstances make it unfair to admit the duplicate. On the face of the rule, then, a forensic image produced and verified as described above is not a lesser form of the evidence. Whether it is admitted in a particular matter remains the court's decision.

8

Reporting

The report states each opinion, the basis and reasons for it, the facts or data considered, and the method applied, in that order, and mapped so that each sentence of conclusion can be traced back to something verified. Where a matter calls for a report under FRCP 26(a)(2)(B), it contains the complete statement of opinions with bases and reasons, the facts or data considered, exhibits, the examiner's qualifications with any publications from the previous ten years, a list of any other cases in which the examiner testified as an expert in the previous four years, and the compensation statement. It is prepared and signed by the examiner who did the work. Timing follows FRCP 26(a)(2)(D) absent a stipulation or a court order, which is to say the default sequence is a default and not a fixture.

The operative admissibility test is the text of FRE 702 as amended effective 1 December 2023. The proponent must show that it is more likely than not that each requirement is met, and 702(d) asks whether the opinion reflects a reliable application of the method to the facts of the case. Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), gloss the rule. Kumho is the governing gloss for digital forensics, which is technical and experience-based, and its factors are flexible rather than a checklist. I check the applicable standard for the venue at engagement, and the condition in section 1 applies to it: where a venue requires a qualification I do not hold, I identify that at scoping and state it in the engagement letter, or I decline the matter. New York, where this practice is based, remains a Frye jurisdiction (Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923); People v. Wesley, 83 N.Y.2d 417 (1994)), and the general-acceptance question is a different question from the 702 one.

I distinguish what I relied on from what is offered as evidence. Third-party logs and vendor telemetry can support an opinion under FRE 703 without themselves being admitted, and FRE 705 governs disclosure of the underlying facts. I also say which kind of exhibit I am producing. A timeline assembled from artefacts is ordinarily an illustrative aid under FRE 107, effective 1 December 2024, and is not itself evidence, whereas a computed summary of voluminous admissible material can be substantive evidence under FRE 1006. Producing one and describing it as the other is an avoidable fight, and I have no appetite for the avoidable ones.

9

Return or destruction

At the close of a matter, evidence is returned or destroyed on written instruction. I re-verify master images on withdrawal from the store and compare the digest recorded at withdrawal against the digest recorded at deposit. Destruction happens against a written instruction only, and I issue a certificate for it. I close the custody record with the same discipline I opened it with, which is the point at which a custody record either reads as a document or reads as an afterthought.

11

What publishing this costs me

A method published at this level of detail can be checked, which means it can also be criticised. That is the trade and I have made it deliberately. An examiner whose procedure is described only in general terms, all "forensically sound" and "court-admissible" and "bulletproof chain of custody", has given you nothing you can test before the engagement and nothing to defend during it.

What it means for you in practice is that I will tell you, before you pay for an attempt, when a thing is unlikely to be recoverable. Deleted-data recovery depends on the medium, the filesystem and the encryption, and on modern encrypted flash storage it is frequently not available at all. An encrypted volume without a key is not opened by effort. A locked modern handset may not be reachable at any price. I will also report findings that do not help you, at the same rate, because the rate does not move with what I find. That is a worse outcome for me and a perfectly good one for you, and it is the reason this is worth commissioning rather than taking anybody's word for it.
