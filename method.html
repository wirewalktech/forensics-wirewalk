1

What this document is

This is the procedure. It is published in full because the procedure is the part of a forensic engagement that gets attacked, and an examiner who has not written it down before the engagement will be writing it on the stand. Nothing here is a claim about past matters. It is a statement of what we do, in enough detail that an opposing examiner could follow the same steps against the same media and say whether our result is reproducible.

Two things this document is not. It is not a guarantee of admissibility — that is the court's decision and nobody can promise it. And it is not a claim of accreditation. The standards named below are guidance we work to, not certificates we hold. Where an engagement requires a certified examiner, an accredited laboratory or a licence, that condition is identified at scoping, stated in the engagement letter and satisfied before work begins, or the matter is declined.

1.1

The four principles the method is written against

ISO/IEC 27037:2012 sets out four requirements for the handling of digital evidence, and every step below exists to satisfy one of them. Auditability — an independent assessor can see what was done and why. Repeatability — the same test on the same material under the same conditions gives the same result. Reproducibility — a different examiner, with different equipment, reaches the same result. Justifiability — every choice, including the choices that altered something, can be explained and defended. The same standard separately states three qualities the evidence itself must have — relevance, reliability and sufficiency — and the four requirements are cited here because they are the ones a procedure can be written against.

Repeatability and reproducibility are the two that decide contested matters. A finding the other side's examiner cannot reproduce is not a finding; it is our word. That is why the working notes record filter criteria, search terms and tool versions rather than conclusions alone.

2

Authority and scope, before anything is touched

The first step is not technical. Before a device is connected to anything, we confirm and record who is authorising access to it, what they are authorising access to, and what entitles them to authorise it. We decline where the authority is unclear, and we would rather lose the engagement at that point than at the point where the other side asks the question.

This is not procedural fastidiousness. Access without authority engages the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030; interception of communications in transit engages the Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2511; and the Stored Communications Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2702, is the reason a civil subpoena to a provider does not produce message content, whatever the subpoena says. State computer-crime and eavesdropping statutes add further constraints and are not uniform. The recurring flashpoints, named so they are dealt with at scoping rather than discovered later: personal devices used for work under a BYOD arrangement; household machines used by more than one person; accounts where a spouse, a relative or a co-owner supplies the credentials; and saved sessions on a company laptop that reach into an individual's personal mail or cloud storage. A credential that works is not authority to use it.

Suspected child sexual abuse material is handled under a stop-and-escalate rule that cannot be varied by agreement: we do not take or retain custody of it, and we work through counsel and law enforcement on access, noting 18 U.S.C. § 3509(m) in federal criminal matters. The full statement is on evidence handling, and clients are told before they engage us rather than afterwards.

2.1

Scope is written down before collection, not inferred from it

We record the question the examination is meant to answer, the custodians and systems in scope, the date range, and what is deliberately excluded. Where a targeted collection answers the question, we take the targeted collection and record in the manifest what we chose not to collect and why. We do not image a whole disk because it is easier to defend than a decision, and we do not bill the processing of volume we did not need.

3

Acquisition

Acquisition is the point at which the evidence either becomes checkable or stops being checkable, and it is almost always unrepeatable. Everything after it is recoverable from a mistake. This is not.

3.1

Write blocking

Where the source permits it, the source is presented to the acquisition system through a hardware write blocker, or through a software write block where hardware is not usable for that interface. Where a software block is used, what validation it has been subjected to, and by whom, is recorded with the rest of the tool record rather than asserted. The blocked state is confirmed and recorded before imaging begins, not assumed from the presence of the device. The make, model and firmware or version of the blocker goes in the acquisition log alongside the imaging tool and its version.

3.2

When write blocking cannot be used — which is often

Write blocking cannot be used for live memory capture, for a running production system that must not be taken down, for most mobile extractions, or for cloud and SaaS sources where the collection is an authenticated API session rather than a read of a medium. In those cases the acquisition alters something, and pretending otherwise is worse than the alteration.

Soundness then comes from four things, all of them recorded at the time: the method is documented step by step; the tool and its version are recorded; the output is hashed at the earliest point at which a hash is meaningful; and the alteration that was unavoidable is stated plainly, with the reason it was unavoidable and an assessment of what it could have affected. A running system changes while you look at it. A report that says so is stronger than one that does not, because the first thing an examiner on the other side will do is find the artefacts our own tooling wrote.

3.3

Live acquisition, and when it is the right call

Live acquisition is the right call when the most valuable evidence will not survive a power-off, or when powering off destroys access to it. Memory is the first case: it holds running processes, network connections, injected code and, in an intrusion, often the only copy of what was executed. It is overwritten continuously while the machine runs and it does not survive power-off at all.

Full-volume encryption is the second and more commonly decisive case. A running, unlocked machine with BitLocker or FileVault engaged is readable. The same machine powered off, without an escrowed recovery key, is a block of ciphertext that no amount of effort or expenditure opens. We establish the encryption state and the key position before anything is powered down, and we capture memory before we capture disk where both are in scope, following the order of volatility in NIST SP 800-86.

The trade is stated rather than hidden: capturing memory from a running system runs code on that system and writes to it. We record what was run, from what medium, at what time, and under what account.

3.4

Mobile and cloud

Mobile and cloud acquisitions are logical and partial. What is obtainable changes with handset, operating system version, security patch level and, for cloud, with the tenant's licence tier and retention configuration at the moment of collection. We record the level achieved, the method used, and — the part that matters later — what was not obtainable at that level. For a cloud collection we also record the authority relied on and the tenant's retention and licence tier as they stood at collection, because both will have changed by the time anyone reads the report.

4

Verification

A digest is computed over the bytes as they are read from the source, in the same pass that writes the image. That is the acquisition digest. A second digest is then computed over the stored image afterwards, as a separate operation, preferably with a different tool from the one that wrote it. That is the verification digest. Only the comparison of the two means anything. A tool that reports its own image as verified using its own arithmetic in its own pass is testing less than it appears to be testing.

The image is re-verified again before analysis begins, and again before anything is produced to another party. SHA-256 is the primary algorithm. The limit belongs in the same paragraph as the claim: a hash match proves the image corresponds to the source as it was read at acquisition. It does not prove the source was unaltered before we arrived, it says nothing about who created any of the data, and it is not a statement about content.

4.2

What a mismatch means, and what we do about it

A mismatch means one of three things, and the whole point of the procedure is to find out which: the source was read differently on the two occasions, usually because it is failing; the image was damaged in transfer or in storage; or something wrote to the image. It is never ignored and it is never quietly re-run until it passes.

When a verification digest does not match, work stops. Nothing is analysed from that image. The failed verification is recorded with its timestamp, both digests and the tool that produced each, and that record goes into the report whether or not the problem is subsequently resolved. We then narrow the discrepancy rather than guess at it: where the image is segmented, per-segment digests localise it to a segment; where the source is still available and still readable, a fresh acquisition is taken and compared; where the medium is failing, we re-acquire with error handling recorded and the bad sectors enumerated, and we say which regions of the image are not reliable.

A failing drive is the common case and it does not destroy the evidence. What destroys the evidence is a second acquisition that silently replaces the first with no record that the first ever existed. Where the source is no longer available and the discrepancy cannot be resolved, we report the discrepancy and the limits it places on every finding downstream of it. That is an unattractive paragraph to write and it is far less unattractive than having it drawn out of us later.

4.3

Why MD5 and SHA-1 still appear

They appear because tools still emit them, because legacy case records contain them, and because a second digest from a second algorithm is a cheap independent check that the image file on disk has not changed. They are recorded where a tool provides them and they are never described as secure. MD5 and SHA-1 both have practical collision attacks; under NIST SP 800-131A Rev. 2 SHA-1 is disallowed for digital signature generation and permitted only for legacy signature verification, and NIST has since announced the transition away from SHA-1 for all applications. Where a digest is doing evidentiary work, the digest doing it is SHA-256.

5

Working copies

Analysis is performed on a working copy. The master image is stored under access control with its own integrity record and is not mounted by an analysis tool. Working copies are hash-verified against the master before use and are discarded and remade rather than repaired if anything about their state is uncertain.

The reason is not superstition about touching originals. It is that an analysis suite is a large body of software that writes caches, indexes and recovery files, and the cost of being wrong about which of those writes touched the evidence file is the whole engagement. A working copy makes the question moot.

6

Examination

Examination proceeds from the scope question recorded at 2.1. We record the tool inventory with versions, the search terms and filter criteria applied, the date ranges and time zones used, and the hypotheses tested — including the ones that were tested and discarded. Findings are stated with the artefacts they rest on, so that another examiner can go to the same artefacts and disagree with our reading of them if they see it differently.

Two disciplines are worth naming because they are where examinations go wrong. Timestamps are treated as evidence about a system's record-keeping, not as facts about the world: they depend on time zone, clock drift, NTP state, filesystem semantics and the behaviour of the application that wrote them, and where two timestamp sources conflict we say so rather than choosing the convenient one. And absence is treated as absence of an artefact, never as evidence that an event did not happen — retention windows, log rotation, TRIM on solid-state media and ordinary system behaviour all destroy data with no intent behind it.

Where we report on destruction, we report what was destroyed, when, and by what mechanism. Routine retention deletion looks a great deal like deliberate destruction in the artefacts, and where the two cannot be distinguished we say that they cannot. Whether a party acted with intent to deprive another of evidence is a finding for the court under FRCP 37(e)(2), and we do not offer it.

6.1

Notes are written on the assumption that they will be produced

Contemporaneous notes, the tool inventory, search terms, filter criteria and discarded hypotheses are kept in a form fit to be handed over, because FRCP 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) reaches the facts or data considered by a testifying expert, not merely those relied upon. Material supplied by counsel is segregated and logged separately, so that the carve-outs in FRCP 26(b)(4)(C) — compensation, facts or data provided and considered, assumptions provided and relied on — can be honoured without sweeping in protected communications or draft reports, which are protected under 26(b)(4)(B).

7

Chain of custody

A written custody record covers receipt, every named custodian, every transfer with date and time, the storage conditions between transfers, and the return or destruction at the end. It is written as the work happens. A custody record reconstructed afterwards from memory and calendar entries is exactly what it looks like.

7.1

The distinction most people get wrong

Authentication is a comparatively low bar. Under FRE 901(a) the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what it is claimed to be — a question of conditional relevance under FRE 104(b), decided on whether a reasonable juror could so find, not on whether the judge is persuaded. Illustrations 901(b)(1), (b)(3), (b)(4) and (b)(9) each supply a route, and gaps in a custody record ordinarily go to weight rather than admissibility (United States v. Howard-Arias, 679 F.2d 363 (4th Cir. 1982)).

That is precisely why the custody record matters more, not less. Evidence that squeaks past the admissibility threshold with an incoherent custody record arrives in front of the fact-finder already damaged, and every hour of cross-examination spent on where the drive was over a weekend is an hour not spent on what was on it. The rule is generous; the jury is not. We do not overstate the rule in our favour, and we do not treat "it will come in anyway" as a reason to keep a thinner record.

The foundation for authentication is built at collection time rather than assembled before trial. That is the structural argument in Lorraine v. Markel American Insurance Co., 241 F.R.D. 534 (D. Md. 2007), and it is the reason the imaging worksheet, the acquisition log and the custody record are produced as the work happens.

7.2

Self-authentication, and what it does not resolve

Two provisions are available and they are routinely stated the wrong way round. FRE 902(14) covers data copied from an electronic device, storage medium or file, authenticated by a process of digital identification — the hash-verified copy. FRE 902(13) covers a record generated by an electronic process or system — a log file. Both require a certification by a qualified person meeting the requirements of FRE 902(11) or (12), and 902(11) carries the notice condition: reasonable written notice of the intent to offer the record, with the record and certification made available for inspection in advance.

The limit, stated where the capability is stated: these provisions remove the need for live foundation testimony on authenticity only. They resolve neither hearsay, nor relevance, nor best evidence, and an opponent remains free to contest authenticity.

On best evidence: FRE 1002 read with 1001(d) and 1001(e) means that for electronically stored information an "original" includes any printout or other output readable by sight that accurately reflects the information, and under FRE 1003 a duplicate is admissible to the same extent as the original unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or the circumstances make it unfair to admit the duplicate. On the face of the rule, then, a forensic image produced and verified as described above is not a lesser form of the evidence. Whether it is admitted in a particular matter remains the court's decision.

8

Reporting

The report states each opinion, the basis and reasons for it, the facts or data considered, and the method applied — in that order, and mapped so that each sentence of conclusion can be traced to something verified. Where a matter calls for a report under FRCP 26(a)(2)(B), it contains the complete statement of opinions with bases and reasons, the facts or data considered, exhibits, the examiner's qualifications with any publications from the previous ten years, a list of any other cases in which the examiner testified as an expert in the previous four years, and the compensation statement; and it is prepared and signed by the examiner who did the work. Timing follows FRCP 26(a)(2)(D) absent a stipulation or a court order, which means the default sequence is a default and not a fixture.

The operative admissibility test is the text of FRE 702 as amended effective 1 December 2023. The proponent must show that it is more likely than not that each requirement is met, and 702(d) asks whether the opinion reflects a reliable application of the method to the facts of the case. Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), gloss the rule; Kumho is the governing gloss for digital forensics, which is technical and experience-based, and its factors are flexible rather than a checklist. The applicable standard is checked for the venue at engagement, and the condition in section 1 applies to it: where a venue requires a qualification we do not hold, that is identified at scoping and stated in the engagement letter, or the matter is declined. New York, where this practice is based, remains a Frye jurisdiction (Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923); People v. Wesley, 83 N.Y.2d 417 (1994)), and the general-acceptance question is a different question from the 702 one.

We distinguish what was relied on from what is offered as evidence. Third-party logs and vendor telemetry can support an opinion under FRE 703 without themselves being admitted, and FRE 705 governs disclosure of the underlying facts. We also say which kind of exhibit we are producing: a timeline assembled from artefacts is ordinarily an illustrative aid under FRE 107, effective 1 December 2024, and is not itself evidence, whereas a computed summary of voluminous admissible material can be substantive evidence under FRE 1006. Producing one and describing it as the other is an avoidable fight.

9

Return or destruction

At the close of a matter, evidence is returned or destroyed on written instruction. Master images are re-verified on withdrawal from the store, and the digest recorded at withdrawal is compared against the digest recorded at deposit. Destruction is performed against written instruction only, and a certificate is issued. The custody record is closed with the same discipline it was opened with, which is the point at which a custody record either reads as a document or reads as an afterthought.

11

What publishing this costs us

A method published at this level of detail can be checked, which means it can also be criticised. That is the trade, and it is deliberate. An examiner whose procedure is described only in general terms — "forensically sound", "court-admissible", "bulletproof chain of custody" — has nothing that can be tested before the engagement and nothing that can be defended during it.

The practical consequence for a client is that we will tell you, before you pay for an attempt, when a thing is unlikely to be recoverable: deleted-data recovery is conditional on medium, filesystem and encryption and is frequently unavailable on modern encrypted flash storage; an encrypted volume without a key is not opened by effort; a locked modern handset may simply not be reachable at any price. We will also report findings that do not help you, at the same rate, because the rate does not move with what we find. That is a worse outcome for us and a perfectly good one for you, and it is the reason this is worth commissioning rather than taking our word for it.
